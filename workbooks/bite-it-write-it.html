Section One
WHY BOTHER WRITING IT DOWN

Ask most people what they ate on Tuesday and they'll give you a version of the truth — reasonable, plausible, and quietly wrong. Not because they're lying. Because memory isn't a filing cabinet. It's more like a story we retell ourselves each time we reach for it, and every retelling smooths the edges a little more.

That's not a flaw in you. It's how memory has always worked, for everyone, since before writing existed. The trouble is that a smoothed-over story is a poor tool for noticing that the 3pm slump always follows the same two foods, or that the good sleep nights line up with the early dinners. Those patterns are small and unglamorous and entirely invisible to a memory that's busy being kind to you.

The food journal isn't about control. It isn't a scoreboard, and it certainly isn't a confession booth. It's closer to a weather log — a plain, dated record of what happened, kept without judgement, so that six weeks from now the pattern can show itself instead of staying buried under a hundred half-remembered Tuesdays.

Write it down. Especially the bad days. A journal that only records the good days is a fiction — and a fiction is exactly what memory would have given you for free.

There's no wrong way to fill a page here. "Ate standing up at the counter, felt fine, felt tired an hour later" is a complete and useful entry. So is "skipped lunch, regretted it by four." The point was never to perform a perfect diet on paper. It was to have an honest witness — even when that witness is only you, an hour later, reading your own handwriting.

Section Two
HOW TO USE THIS JOURNAL

Six honest rules — none of them difficult, all of them worth actually following.

Write it as you go
Not from memory at the end of the day
The three-word description written the moment you eat it beats the beautifully reconstructed memory written at 9pm.
Note the time
It matters more than it seems
Energy dips and food are often separated by an hour or two — long enough that the connection disappears unless the clock is written down alongside the food.
Rate the energy after, honestly
Not the energy you expected to have
Fill in the dots an hour or two after eating, not immediately. The immediate feeling is mostly digestion.
Review weekly, not daily
The pattern is invisible day by day
One day tells you almost nothing. Seven days, read together, tend to tell you a great deal.
Leave nothing out
Especially the things you'd rather not write
The occasional biscuit, the second coffee, the meal skipped entirely — all of it is data. None of it is a confession.
Trust the slow reveal
Patterns take a fortnight, not a day
Most people notice their first real pattern somewhere around day ten to fourteen. Keep going even if early pages feel uneventful.

That's the whole method. Print the days that follow, or fill them in here — one page per day, thirty days, a weekly review after each seven.
