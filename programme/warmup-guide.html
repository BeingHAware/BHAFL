Every movement in the Foundation Level warm-up — explained in full, with frame sequences, step-by-step instruction, and Common Folly corrections. No assumptions. No guessing. Just the correct way to prepare the body for the work ahead.

Why the warm-up matters
The warm-up is not optional and it is not a formality. It is the first ten minutes of every session — the period in which the joints lubricate, the nervous system wakes up, and the body reveals what it has been doing (or not doing) since the last session. A body that goes straight into working sets from cold is a body that will tell you about it eventually. Begin here. Every time.

01
Phase One · 3 minutes
JOINTS FIRST
Synovial fluid, range of motion, and the five joints that need waking before anything else moves

Joints move differently when warm. Synovial fluid — the lubricating liquid inside every joint — becomes less viscous with movement and heat, allowing the joint surfaces to glide more smoothly and with less resistance. Starting with joint circles before any muscular work is not ceremony. It is the body's own maintenance procedure, performed in the correct order.

Joints First · Movement 01 of 05
NECK CIRCLES
Slow, controlled rotation through the cervical spine — the joint most compressed by desk work, screens, and sleep position
Cervical spine10 each directionSlow and controlled
Frame 1 — Starting Position
Sitting or standing tall. Shoulders relaxed and away from the ears. Eyes forward. The neck is in neutral — not pushed forward, not pulled back. This is the position you return to between each circle.
What It Does
Primary
Lubricates the cervical (neck) joints with synovial fluid. Releases tension accumulated from sleep, screens, or a day of looking down. Restores the full range of motion the neck was designed to have.
Secondary
Gently activates the deep neck stabilisers. Creates proprioceptive awareness — the body beginning to understand where it is in space before the session begins.
Step by Step
1
Sit or stand tall
Shoulders down and back, away from the ears. Spine long. Do not let the shoulders creep up as the neck moves — this is the most common compensation.
2
Drop the chin slowly to the chest
Let gravity do the work. No forcing. Feel the stretch along the back of the neck.
Feel it: gentle pull along the back of the neck and upper back
3
Roll slowly to one ear
The ear moves toward the shoulder — the shoulder does not move toward the ear. Slow. Controlled. Three seconds per quarter turn.
4
Continue the circle
Back of the head toward the upper back (a gentle backward tilt — not a full extension), across to the other ear, and back to chin-to-chest. That is one circle. Complete 10, then reverse direction for 10 more.
Feel it: each quadrant revealing where the stiffness lives
Common Folly
Common Folly — Neck Circles
Moving too fast — spinning through the range rather than exploring it.
Slow down to the point where you can feel each degree of movement. The therapeutic value is in the slow exploration of range, not the completion of laps. Three seconds per quarter turn is the target.
Shoulders rising as the neck moves — the upper traps doing the work the neck should be doing.
Consciously press the shoulders down before beginning and check them every few circles. If they've crept up, reset. The neck moves independently of the shoulders.
Forcing range at the stiff points — pushing through resistance rather than breathing into it.
Tightness is information. Pause at the stiff point, breathe out, and let the release happen rather than pushing through it. Forcing creates guarding. Breathing creates opening.
Note
If you experience dizziness, sharp pain, or any neurological sensation (tingling, numbness) during neck circles, stop immediately and reduce the range of motion to gentle side-to-side nods only. These sensations warrant attention from a healthcare professional before continuing full circles.
Joints First · Movement 02 of 05
SHOULDER ROLLS
The shoulder girdle — the most complex joint in the body, and the one most compressed by modern posture
Shoulder girdle10 each directionFull range
Frame 1 — Starting Position
Standing or seated, arms relaxed by the sides. Shoulders in whatever position they naturally fall — for most people this will be slightly forward and elevated. The roll will correct this by moving through the full range.
What It Does
Primary
Lubricates the glenohumeral (ball and socket) joint, the acromioclavicular joint, and the sternoclavicular joint — the three joints that make up the shoulder complex. All three need movement before load.
Secondary
Activates and reminds the lower trapezius — the muscle responsible for pulling the shoulder blades down, which is the initiating movement of every pull exercise in the programme.
Step by Step
1
Lift both shoulders toward the ears
An exaggerated shrug — as high as the shoulders will go. This is the starting point of the forward roll.
2
Roll them forward and down
Draw the shoulders forward (hunching slightly), then down toward the floor. Feel the front of the chest opening as the shoulders descend.
Feel it: the shoulder blades spreading apart as the shoulders come forward
3
Draw them back and up to complete the circle
Pull the shoulder blades together and back, then lift back to the starting shrug position. That is one forward roll. Complete 10, then reverse — back, down, forward, up — for 10 more.
Feel it: the shoulder blades pinching together on the backward phase
Common Folly
Common Folly — Shoulder Rolls
Small, lazy rolls that barely move the joint — completing the number without earning the range.
Make the roll as large as possible. Exaggerate each phase. The ears-to-shoulders shrug, the full forward hunch, the back-pinch, the full lift. Small rolls produce small benefit.
Rolling both shoulders at the same time in a way that doesn't actually move the shoulder blades.
Focus on feeling the shoulder blades move — spreading apart on the forward phase, pinching together on the backward phase. If you can't feel the blades moving, slow down and make the movement bigger.
Why This Matters
The shoulder roll is the warm-up movement most likely to be done correctly when someone is told to do it slowly — and most likely to be done uselessly when they're in a hurry. It takes thirty seconds done properly. It prepares the joint for every push, pull, and overhead movement in the session. The thirty seconds is not optional.
Joints First · Movement 03 of 05
WRIST ROTATIONS
The joint that bears the load in every push exercise — and the one most neglected before it is asked to work
Wrist and forearm10 each directionBoth hands
Frame 1 — Starting Position
Arms extended forward or hands clasped together in front of the chest. The wrist is in neutral — not flexed or extended. Both wrists will rotate together or separately depending on preference.
What It Does
Primary
Lubricates the radiocarpal and intercarpal joints of the wrist. Warms the eight small carpal bones and the connective tissue that holds them together. Essential before any weight-bearing through the hands.
Secondary
Activates the forearm flexors and extensors. Identifies any existing tightness or restriction before it becomes a problem under load in the push-up or dip.
Step by Step
1
Clasp the hands or extend arms forward
Fingers interlaced and palms facing away from you for the clasped version, or arms extended with hands open for the individual version. Either works.
2
Rotate slowly through the full range
Draw large, slow circles with the wrists — clockwise for 10 repetitions. The movement comes from the wrist, not the elbow or shoulder.
Feel it: the small joints of the wrist working through their full available range
3
Reverse for 10 more
Anti-clockwise. Same slow, full circles. Note whether one direction is more restricted than the other — this is useful information.
4
Finish with wrist flexion and extension
Hands open, fingers pointing up — press gently back with the other hand for a flexor stretch. Then fingers pointing down — press gently for an extensor stretch. Five seconds each. These are the positions the wrist will be in under load.
Feel it: the forearm muscles stretching in both directions
Common Folly
Common Folly — Wrist Rotations
Skipping wrist rotations entirely — treating them as optional because the wrists "feel fine."
The wrist feels fine until it doesn't — and the moment it doesn't is usually mid-set in a push-up or dip. Thirty seconds of wrist circles costs nothing. A wrist injury costs weeks. Do the circles.
Rotating from the elbow rather than the wrist — the whole forearm rotating rather than just the wrist joint.
Keep the forearm still. Only the wrist moves. If the whole arm is rotating, reduce the range and focus on the joint itself rather than the appearance of movement.
Particularly Important For
Anyone whose work involves typing, gripping tools, or repetitive hand movements. The wrist tends to accumulate tension and restriction that is invisible until load is applied. The warm-up rotation makes it visible at a safe moment — before the session rather than during it.
Joints First · Movement 04 of 05
HIP CIRCLES
The largest ball-and-socket joint in the body — restricted by sitting, loaded by every lower body movement in the programme
Hip joint10 each directionStanding
Frame 1 — Starting Position
Standing with feet hip-width apart, hands on the hips. Slight softness in the knees — not locked out. This movement comes from the hip joint, not the lower back.
What It Does
Primary
Lubricates the hip joint — the largest ball-and-socket joint in the body. Warms the acetabular labrum (the cartilage ring that deepens the hip socket) and the surrounding ligaments before they are loaded under the squat, lunge, and glute bridge.
Secondary
Reveals hip mobility restrictions early. A hip that catches, clicks, or resists in one direction of the circle during the warm-up is a hip that needs attention — not avoidance.
Step by Step
1
Stand with feet hip-width apart
Hands on hips. Soft knees. Spine neutral. The movement originates at the hip — the pelvis moves, not the torso.
2
Draw a large circle with the hips
Push the hips forward, then to one side, then back (sticking the tailbone out), then to the other side, and back to forward. Think of stirring a large pot with the hips. 10 slow circles.
Feel it: the hip joint moving in all planes — forward, side, back, side
3
Reverse for 10 more
The reverse direction often feels more restricted. This is normal. The restriction is the reason you are doing this movement.
Feel it: note which direction and which point in the circle feels tightest
Common Folly
Common Folly — Hip Circles
Moving the torso rather than the hips — swaying from side to side from the waist rather than rotating the hip joint itself.
Keep the shoulders still and let only the pelvis move. Place the hands on the hip bones and feel them moving in the circle — not the ribs, not the shoulders. Smaller movement from the right joint is better than large movement from the wrong one.
Making the circles too small to produce any joint lubrication.
The circle should be as large as the hip joint's current range allows. Push to the edges — gently, with control — not to a comfortable small rotation that asks nothing of the joint. The edges are where the lubrication is needed most.
For Those Who Sit for Long Periods
The hip flexors shorten and the posterior capsule of the hip joint tightens with prolonged sitting. Hip circles are the minimum daily maintenance for anyone who spends significant time at a desk. If the circles feel very restricted, consider adding the hip flexor stretch (Phase 2) before this movement as well as after it.
Joints First · Movement 05 of 05
ANKLE CIRCLES
The foundation of every squat, lunge, and step — and the joint most likely to be the hidden limit of lower body range
Ankle joint10 each directionBoth ankles
Frame 1 — Seated or Standing
Seated with one foot raised, or standing with the heel on the floor and the toes lifted. The ankle hangs freely enough to rotate through its full range. This can also be done standing on one leg with the other foot lifted.
What It Does
Primary
Lubricates the talocrural joint (the main ankle joint) and the subtalar joint beneath it. Warms the Achilles tendon and the plantar fascia. Ankle dorsiflexion — the ability to bring the shin toward the foot — limits squat depth when restricted. This addresses it.
Secondary
Activates the small stabilising muscles of the lower leg and foot. Improves proprioception — the sense of where the foot is in space, which determines balance in every single-leg movement.
Step by Step
1
Lift one foot off the floor
Seated, or standing and balancing on the other leg. The lifted foot hangs freely. The movement comes from the ankle — not the knee, not the hip.
2
Draw large slow circles with the foot
Clockwise for 10 repetitions. Think of drawing circles with the big toe — large circles, through the full available range. Point the toes fully at the bottom, flex them back at the top.
Feel it: the full range of the ankle — pointed, to the outside, flexed, to the inside
3
Reverse and repeat on the other side
Anti-clockwise for 10, then switch feet and repeat both directions. Note any difference between left and right — asymmetry here explains a great deal about what happens in the squat.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Ankle Circles
Rotating from the knee rather than the ankle — the whole lower leg swinging rather than just the foot.
Keep the knee still. Only the foot moves. If the knee is swinging, the ankle is not doing the work. Hold the shin with one hand to stabilise if needed and feel the ankle rotating independently.
Ignoring the asymmetry between left and right ankles.
If one ankle is significantly more restricted than the other, it will limit that side in every squat, lunge, and single-leg exercise. Note which side and spend an additional 10 circles on the restricted side. Over time, address the restriction with calf stretching and dorsiflexion mobility work.
The Squat Connection
Restricted ankle dorsiflexion is one of the most common hidden causes of the heel rising in a squat. If the squat section of the programme reveals that your heels come up before you reach depth, the ankle circle warm-up is not optional — it is the beginning of solving the problem. Add wall ankle dorsiflexion stretches after the session as well.

Phase Two · 3 minutes
HINGE & REACH
Spine mobility, hip flexibility, and the positions that reveal where the body has been holding tension

The hinge and reach phase takes the joints that were lubricated in Phase One and moves them through their full functional range. This is where the spine is mobilised, the hip flexors are addressed, and the body's accumulated stiffness from sitting, sleeping, and screen time begins to release. Move slowly. Breathe deliberately. Notice what you find.

Hinge and Reach · Movement 01 of 04
CAT-COW
Spinal flexion and extension in alternation — the spine's own morning routine, borrowed from yoga and earned by everyone
Thoracic and lumbar spine10 full cyclesOn hands and knees
Frame 1 — Neutral Table Position
On hands and knees — wrists under shoulders, knees under hips. The spine is in neutral — not arched, not rounded. This is the starting and ending position of every cycle. It is also a position worth knowing on its own.
What It Does
Primary
Mobilises the entire spine through flexion (the Cat — rounded back) and extension (the Cow — arched back). Pumps synovial fluid through the intervertebral discs. Releases the deep spinal muscles that have been held in one position during sleep or sitting.
Secondary
Develops awareness of the difference between a posteriorly tilted pelvis (Cat position) and an anteriorly tilted pelvis (Cow position). This awareness is directly relevant to the hollow body hold and every push exercise in the programme.
Step by Step
1
Begin on hands and knees — neutral spine
Wrists directly under shoulders. Knees directly under hips. Spine neither arched nor rounded. Head in line with the spine, eyes looking at the floor.
2
CAT — exhale and round the spine
Breathe out, tuck the tailbone under, round the lower back, pull the navel toward the spine, and let the upper back dome upward. The head drops naturally. Think of an angry cat arching its back.
Feel it: the entire spine rounding from tailbone to neck — one continuous curve
3
COW — inhale and arch the spine
Breathe in, let the belly drop toward the floor, lift the tailbone, and allow the lower back to arch gently. The head rises to look forward. Think of a cow's sagging belly.
Feel it: the front of the spine lengthening, the lower back gently arching
4
Flow between Cat and Cow
Move continuously from one to the other, led by the breath. Exhale into Cat. Inhale into Cow. Let the breath dictate the pace rather than counting. 10 full cycles.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Cat-Cow
Moving only the lower back — the thoracic spine (upper and mid back) staying stiff and uninvolved.
Think of the movement beginning at the tailbone and travelling all the way to the base of the skull, one vertebra at a time. The upper back must participate. If it feels stuck, slow down and breathe into the stiff area — the thoracic spine is often the most immobile section in people who sit at desks.
Rushing through the movement without connecting the breath to the position.
The breath is the movement. Exhale drives Cat, inhale drives Cow. When the breath is disconnected, the movement becomes mechanical and loses most of its therapeutic value. Slow down until the breath leads and the body follows.
Forcing the Cow position into an excessive lower back arch.
Cow is a gentle extension — the belly softly dropping, the tailbone lifting. It is not a maximum lumbar hyperextension. If the lower back feels compressed in Cow, reduce the range. A modest arch is enough.
The Hollow Body Connection
The Cat position is the spinal shape that the hollow body hold works to create. The ability to round the lower back deliberately — to feel the difference between an arched and a flat lumbar spine — is the foundational skill of the hollow body hold. Cat-cow teaches this distinction clearly, in a low-load position, before any demand is placed on the core.
Hinge and Reach · Movement 02 of 04
CHILD'S POSE
The resting extension that decompresses the lower back and opens the hips simultaneously — 30 seconds of genuine recovery
Lower back and hipsHold 30 secondsOn the floor
Frame 1 — Starting Position
From the cat-cow table position — on hands and knees, neutral spine. Child's pose begins from here, so it flows naturally from the previous movement without getting up.
What It Does
Primary
Passively lengthens the lumbar erectors (lower back muscles), the latissimus dorsi (the large back muscle used in all pulling), and the hip flexors simultaneously. Decompresses the intervertebral discs of the lower back through gentle traction.
Secondary
Provides a moment of genuine stillness before the session begins. The parasympathetic nervous system activates briefly, reducing baseline tension in the muscles that are about to be worked.
Step by Step
1
From hands and knees, sit the hips back toward the heels
Let the hips descend slowly toward the heels. The arms remain extended forward, hands on the floor. The forehead rests on the floor or hovers just above it.
2
Let the body relax completely into the position
The lower back softens and releases. The chest moves gently toward the floor with each exhale. Do not hold tension anywhere in the body. This is a passive stretch — gravity does the work.
Feel it: the lower back releasing, the lats lengthening along the sides of the torso
3
Hold for 30 seconds and breathe
Breathe slowly and deeply. With each exhale, allow the body to sink slightly further into the position. Do not force or bounce. 30 seconds of genuine release.
Feel it: each exhale allowing a little more release in the lower back and hips
Common Folly
Common Folly — Child's Pose
Hips not reaching the heels — staying in a half-way position that stretches nothing.
If the hips won't reach the heels — common with tight hip flexors or knees that protest — place a folded blanket or cushion between the hips and the heels to close the gap. The hips should be resting on something, not hovering in mid-air under muscular tension.
Holding muscular tension throughout — treating it as an active exercise rather than a passive release.
Let go. Completely. The arms are on the floor, the forehead is on the floor, the hips are moving toward the heels under gravity. Nothing should be working. If something is working, find it and release it.
Modification
Wide-knee child's pose: instead of knees together, take the knees wide (toward the edges of the mat) and bring the big toes to touch. The hips sink between the knees rather than on top of them. This variation is significantly more accessible for people with tight hips and produces a deeper inner groin stretch alongside the lower back release.
Hinge and Reach · Movement 03 of 04
DOWNWARD DOG
The whole-body stretch that is also the starting position of the pike push-up — two purposes, one movement
Full posterior chainHold 30 secondsFloor
Frame 1 — From Child's Pose
Coming up from child's pose — hands still on the floor, toes curling under. The body will press back and up into the inverted V of downward dog from here. This transition is itself a movement worth doing slowly.
What It Does
Primary
Stretches the entire posterior chain simultaneously — hamstrings, calves, Achilles, and spinal erectors. Also stretches the lats and the axilla (armpit area) as the arms reach overhead in the inverted position.
Secondary
Activates the shoulder stabilisers in a weight-bearing overhead position — preparation for every push exercise. Also introduces a mild inversion (heart above head) which briefly increases blood flow to the upper body.
Step by Step
1
From child's pose, tuck the toes under
Curl the toes so the balls of the feet are on the floor. Hands remain shoulder-width apart, fingers spread, weight distributed across the whole palm.
2
Press the floor away and lift the hips
Push through the hands and straighten the arms as the hips rise toward the ceiling. The body forms an inverted V — hips at the apex, hands and feet as the two base points.
Feel it: the hamstrings beginning to stretch as the hips rise
3
Press the heels toward the floor
The heels do not need to touch the floor — they probably won't at first. The intention of pressing them down is enough to stretch the calves and Achilles. Do not force the heels down by rounding the lower back.
Feel it: the calf stretch and Achilles lengthening as the heel reaches toward the floor
4
Hold 30 seconds and breathe
With each exhale, let the hips rise slightly and the heels drop slightly further. The arms stay straight, the head hangs freely between the arms, the back of the neck relaxes.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Downward Dog
Rounding the lower back to get the heels closer to the floor — prioritising the appearance over the purpose.
Keep the lower back long and the hips hinging at the top of the V. Bent knees are completely acceptable if they allow the back to stay straight. A straight-back downward dog with bent knees stretches the right things. A rounded-back version with straight legs stretches the wrong things.
Arms bending — weight collapsing into the shoulders rather than being pressed away through straight arms.
Push the floor actively away from you throughout the hold. The arms stay straight and the shoulders push up and back away from the hands. This is also the shoulder preparation for the push-up — practise it here before load is applied.
Head dropping too far — neck craning downward under its own weight.
The head hangs freely between the arms — ears in line with the biceps, same as in the hollow body hold overhead position. It should feel like an extension of the spine, not a separate heavy object pulling downward.
The Pike Push-Up Connection
Downward dog is precisely the starting position of the pike push-up. Familiarising the body with this shape in the warm-up — the high hips, the straight arms, the shoulder loading — means the pike push-up begins from a position the body already knows rather than one it is discovering under load.
Hinge and Reach · Movement 04 of 04
HIP FLEXOR STRETCH
The most shortened muscle group in modern life — and the one whose tightness explains the most about lower back pain, anterior pelvic tilt, and squat restriction
Hip flexors and psoas30 seconds each sideKneeling lunge
Frame 1 — Kneeling Lunge Position
One knee on the floor, one foot forward with the shin vertical. The rear knee is directly below the rear hip. This is the starting position — the stretch begins from here as the hips are driven gently forward.
What It Does
Primary
Stretches the iliopsoas — the deepest and most powerful hip flexor, connecting the lumbar spine to the femur. Shortened iliopsoas is the primary cause of anterior pelvic tilt (the forward-tilted pelvis that creates a pronounced lower back arch) and contributes significantly to lower back pain.
Secondary
Stretches the rectus femoris (the quad muscle that also crosses the hip). Also familiarises the body with the lunge position that appears in the reverse lunge and Bulgarian split squat exercises.
Step by Step
1
Take a kneeling lunge position
One knee on the floor, one foot forward. Front shin vertical. Both hips facing forward. Place the hands on the front knee for balance if needed.
2
Tuck the tailbone under
Before driving the hips forward, posteriorly tilt the pelvis — the same movement as the hollow body hold. This is crucial: without the pelvic tuck, the stretch goes to the lower back rather than the hip flexor.
Feel it: the lower back flattening as the tailbone tucks
3
Drive the hips forward and down
Maintaining the pelvic tuck, shift the whole body forward until the stretch is felt in the front of the rear hip and thigh. The front knee tracks over the toes. Hold 30 seconds.
Feel it: the deep front-of-hip stretch in the rear leg — the iliopsoas releasing
4
Switch sides and repeat
30 seconds each side. If one side is significantly tighter than the other, add a second hold on the tighter side. The asymmetry is worth addressing — it will show up in every lower body exercise.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Hip Flexor Stretch
Not tucking the pelvis — going straight into the forward drive with an anteriorly tilted pelvis.
The pelvic tuck is the whole point. Without it, the stretch goes to the lumbar spine rather than the iliopsoas. Tuck the tailbone first, feel the lower back flatten, then drive forward. The stretch you feel will be completely different — deeper in the front of the hip, absent in the lower back.
The front knee drifting inward or forward past the toes.
The front shin stays vertical throughout. If the knee drifts forward, step the front foot further forward. The same rule applies here as in the reverse lunge — vertical shin, planted heel.
Rushing through — treating 30 seconds as too long.
The hip flexor is a slow-twitch, deeply embedded muscle. It requires time to release. 30 seconds is the minimum. For people who sit for long periods, 60 seconds per side is more appropriate. The tight hip flexor is one of the most consequential restrictions in the entire musculoskeletal system. Give it the time it deserves.
Why This Matters So Much
Chronically shortened hip flexors pull the lumbar spine into extension and the pelvis into anterior tilt — the forward-tilted, protruding-lower-back position that is endemic in desk workers. This creates lower back pain, inhibits glute activation, and restricts squat depth. The hip flexor stretch is the single most important warm-up movement for anyone who sits for significant periods of the day. It deserves more than a perfunctory 10 seconds.

Phase Three · 2 minutes
ACTIVATE
Waking the muscles that support everything else — glutes, deep core, and the scapular stabilisers

The activation phase takes the joints and tissues prepared in Phases One and Two and begins to fire the specific muscles that the session demands. Three movements — glute bridge, dead bug, and scapular pulls — each targeting a muscle group that commonly underperforms when called upon suddenly in a working set. Activating them here costs two minutes. The alternative is discovering mid-session that they weren't ready.

Activate · Movement 01 of 03
GLUTE BRIDGE
15 reps in the warm-up — not for strength, but to ensure the glutes are firing before any lower body exercise demands them
Glute activation15 reps2-second hold at top
Why It Is Here
The glutes are the largest and most powerful muscle group in the lower body. They are also the muscle group most likely to be inhibited — switched off — by prolonged sitting. A glute that is not firing in a squat, lunge, or hip extension exercise means the lower back, hamstrings, or knees are compensating. Fifteen glute bridge reps in the warm-up, with the deliberate two-second squeeze at the top, ensures the glutes are communicating with the nervous system before the session demands them.

"Feel it in the glutes. If you don't feel it in the glutes, adjust until you do — then continue."

Being HAware — Exercise Key
In the warm-up context: the same movement as the full exercise, but lighter in intention. Focus entirely on the sensation of the glutes contracting at the top. This is not a strength set — it is a conversation with the nervous system. Each rep asks the glute to fire. Each squeeze confirms it is listening.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Warm-Up Glute Bridge
Treating the warm-up glute bridge as a strength exercise — going heavy on the squeeze and fatiguing the muscle before the session.
This is an activation movement, not a working set. The squeeze should be firm enough to confirm the glute is firing — not a maximum-effort contraction. Think 60% effort, full attention.
Skipping the two-second hold and bouncing through the reps.
The hold is the activation. A glute bridge without the hold at the top is a hip flexor exercise. The glute needs to be held in contraction briefly for the nervous system to register the pattern. Two seconds, every rep, no exceptions.

Activate · Movement 02 of 03
DEAD BUG
8 each side in the warm-up — waking the deep core stabilisers that protect the spine throughout every exercise that follows
Deep core activation8 each sideSlow
Why It Is Here
The dead bug activates the transverse abdominis — the deepest layer of the core, the one responsible for creating intra-abdominal pressure that stabilises the spine under load. This muscle is not recruited by sit-ups, crunches, or most conventional core exercises. It is recruited by the specific challenge of keeping the lower back flat against the floor while the limbs create an extending force. Eight slow reps each side in the warm-up ensures it is engaged before the session asks it to work.
The one rule: lower back flat against the floor throughout. If it lifts, the activation has stopped. Reduce the range of the limbs until the back stays down, then continue.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Warm-Up Dead Bug
Moving too quickly to feel anything — the warm-up dead bug becoming a mechanical leg-waving exercise.
Four seconds per extension. Feel the back on the floor before each rep. Feel it staying there as the limbs move. Slow is the exercise.

Activate · Movement 03 of 03
SCAPULAR PULLS
10 reps from a hang — the shoulder blade depression that initiates every pull exercise and protects the shoulder joint
Scapular stabilisers10 repsPull-up bar
Why It Is Here
The scapular pull — depressing the shoulder blades from a dead hang without bending the elbows — is the initiating movement of every pull exercise: the inverted row, the chin-up, and the pull-up. Performing 10 slow reps in the warm-up achieves two things simultaneously. It activates the lower trapezius and serratus anterior — the muscles responsible for pulling the shoulder blades down and stabilising them against the ribcage. And it reminds the nervous system of the correct pulling pattern before heavier pulling begins.
If no pull-up bar is available: substitute with a resistance band pull-apart (hold the band at shoulder width and pull it apart to chest width, squeezing the shoulder blades together) for 10–15 reps. The scapular activation is the goal — the bar is the preferred tool, not the only one.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Warm-Up Scapular Pulls
Bending the elbows — turning the scapular pull into a partial pull-up.
Arms stay completely straight throughout. The movement is only in the shoulder blades — down and back, then releasing back to the passive shrug. If the elbows bend, the wrong muscles are doing the work.

Phase Four · 2 minutes
ELEVATE
Heart rate up, blood moving, body temperature rising — the transition from preparation to work

The final phase of the warm-up elevates the heart rate gently and raises core body temperature. A warmer muscle is a more pliable, faster-contracting, and more injury-resistant muscle. Two minutes here is the difference between a body that starts cold and a body that starts ready. Three movements — none of them complicated, all of them effective.

Elevate · Movement 01 of 03
MARCH OR LIGHT JOG
2 minutes on the spot — the simplest cardiovascular primer available, requiring nothing except the willingness to begin
Cardiovascular primer2 minutesOn the spot
What It Does
Raises the heart rate from resting to light aerobic activity. Increases blood flow to the working muscles. Elevates core body temperature — which increases the speed of metabolic processes in the muscles and reduces the viscosity of both synovial fluid and intramuscular connective tissue. A warmer muscle generates force more quickly, absorbs force more safely, and recovers more efficiently.
March vs Jog
March: knees driving up to hip height, arms swinging opposite to the legs. Low impact, suitable for any fitness level, appropriate for sessions immediately after waking or on very fatigued days.

Light jog: easy pace, feet just clearing the floor, breathing comfortable throughout. A gentle conversation should still be possible. If breathless after 30 seconds, drop back to a march.
Common Folly
Common Folly — March or Jog
Going too hard — treating this as a cardio session rather than a primer.
The Elevate phase should leave the body warm and ready — not fatigued. If the legs are heavy by the end of the warm-up, the working sets will suffer. Light means light. The target is a gentle increase in heart rate and temperature, not cardiovascular training.
Skipping this phase because it feels unnecessary after the previous three phases.
The joints are lubricated and the muscles are activated — but the heart rate has not yet elevated and the core temperature has not yet risen. These are different systems. The march or jog addresses specifically what the first three phases do not.
Elevate · Movement 02 of 03
ARM SWINGS
10 front-to-back and 10 cross-body — the dynamic shoulder warm-up that adds movement range to the static preparation of the rolls
Shoulder dynamic mobility10 each patternStanding
What It Does
Takes the shoulder through its full dynamic range using momentum rather than static holding. This is distinct from the shoulder rolls (which lubricated the joint) and prepares the shoulder for the dynamic demands of push and pull exercises — movements that require the joint to decelerate and accelerate through range under load.
Step by Step
1
Front-to-back swings
Stand with feet shoulder-width. Swing both arms forward to chest height then back behind the body, allowing the chest to open on the backswing. Let momentum carry the arms — no muscular forcing. 10 swings.
Feel it: the chest opening and the shoulder joint moving freely through its sagittal range
2
Cross-body swings
Arms swing out to the sides then cross in front of the chest — one arm over the other, alternating which arm crosses on top. 10 swings. The shoulder blades spread apart on the cross and pinch together on the open.
Feel it: the posterior shoulder and upper back opening as the arms cross in front
Common Folly
Common Folly — Arm Swings
Swinging too vigorously — creating impact and jarring at the end range of each swing.
Dynamic mobility means controlled momentum — not uncontrolled flinging. The arms accelerate through the middle of the swing and decelerate at the end range. If the shoulder is jolting at the end of each swing, reduce the amplitude.
Note
Arm swings are particularly useful before sessions that emphasise push or pull movements. On lower-body focused sessions, they can be reduced to 5 of each pattern. On upper-body days, 15 of each is appropriate.
Elevate · Movement 03 of 03
BODYWEIGHT SQUATS
10 deliberate reps — not for strength, but to confirm that everything prepared in the previous three phases is working together correctly
Full lower body primer10 repsSlow and deliberate
Why It Closes the Warm-Up
The bodyweight squat uses every joint mobilised and every muscle activated in the previous three phases simultaneously. The ankles dorsiflex. The hips hinge. The glutes engage. The deep core stabilises. The thoracic spine stays upright. Ten slow, deliberate reps at the end of the warm-up is a full-system check — confirming that the joints are ready, the muscles are firing, and the body is prepared to begin working.
Warm-up squat vs working squat: the warm-up squat is slower and more exploratory. Pause at the bottom. Feel the glutes. Feel the ankle. Feel where the weight sits through the foot. These are the cues you bring into the working sets. The warm-up squats are where you find them, not where you prove them.
What to Notice
Use these 10 reps as a diagnostic. The warm-up squat tells you what today's session will reveal.
What the squat reveals
Heels rising — ankle dorsiflexion is still restricted. More ankle circles and calf stretching needed before lower body work.
Elevate the heels slightly for working sets today, and add ankle mobility work at the end of the session.
Knees caving inward — glutes not yet firing despite the activation phase.
Add 10 more glute bridge reps with a 3-second hold at the top before beginning working sets.
Forward trunk lean — hip flexors still tight or ankle restriction compensating.
Return to the hip flexor stretch for an additional 30 seconds each side. Then retest.
You are ready
Ten minutes. Joints lubricated. Spine mobilised. Hip flexors addressed. Glutes firing. Core active. Scapulae ready. Heart rate elevated. Temperature rising. The session begins from here — from a body that has been asked to prepare, and has done so. Every working set that follows is better for these ten minutes. Every session skipped without them carries a small and accumulating cost. Begin the warm-up. Every time.
