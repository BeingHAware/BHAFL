THE MIGHTY 90
Dr Joel Wallach's 90 essential nutrients — the complete picture
The body requires 90 essential nutrients to function optimally. Not as a rough guideline — as a biological fact. Dr Joel Wallach, whose veterinary and human clinical research spans more than four decades and whose zoological contributions are preserved in the Smithsonian Institution, identified these 90 through comparative study across species: when an animal was denied any one of them consistently, a predictable and specific set of diseases followed. Every time. The implication for human health is profound and largely unacknowledged by mainstream medicine.
The 90 essential nutrients divide into four categories: 60 minerals, 16 vitamins, 12 amino acids, and 3 essential fatty acids. Essential means the body cannot manufacture them — they must come from outside. The modern diet, even a careful one, struggles to deliver all 60 minerals with any consistency. This is not a personal failing. It is a consequence of depleted soils, long supply chains, and the industrialisation of food.

"Every man, woman, and child needs 90 essential nutrients just to survive — let alone thrive."

Dr Joel Wallach — Dead Doctors Don't Lie

The minerals are the most neglected of the 90 and the most consequential. Unlike vitamins, minerals are entirely earth-derived. They enter the food chain through soil, through plants that absorb them, and through the animals that eat those plants. When soil is depleted — as McCance and Widdowson's data confirms it has been since the mid-twentieth century — deficiency cascades are inevitable. Colloidal minerals — plant-derived from ancient deposits — offer significantly higher bioavailability than metallic mineral supplements. The form matters as much as the quantity.
Calcium
Bone density, muscle contraction, nerve signalling, blood clotting. Most abundant mineral in the body.
Dairy, sardines with bones, leafy greens, sesame. Requires D3 and K2 to be directed to bone correctly.
Deficiency: osteoporosis, muscle cramps, poor dental health, hypertension.
Magnesium
Over 300 enzymatic reactions. Muscle relaxation, sleep quality, blood pressure regulation, ATP energy production.
Leafy greens, pumpkin seeds, dark chocolate, almonds. Glycinate or malate form — oxide does not absorb meaningfully.
Deficiency: insomnia, muscle cramps, anxiety, constipation, hypertension. Extremely common.
Potassium
Fluid balance, nerve function, muscle contraction, blood pressure counterbalance to sodium.
Avocado, sweet potato, banana, leafy greens, lentils. Processed food destroys the sodium-potassium ratio.
Deficiency: muscle weakness, heart arrhythmia, fatigue, elevated blood pressure.
Selenium
Antioxidant defence via selenoproteins, thyroid hormone metabolism, immune function, DNA repair.
Brazil nuts (2–3 daily is sufficient and safe), seafood, organ meats. Soil concentration varies enormously by region.
Deficiency: thyroid dysfunction, weakened immunity, Keshan disease, increased cancer susceptibility.
Zinc
Immune function, wound healing, testosterone production, taste and smell, protein synthesis, DNA repair.
Oysters, red meat, pumpkin seeds, chickpeas. Picolinate or bisglycinate absorb well. Excess zinc depletes copper.
Deficiency: impaired immunity, poor wound healing, loss of taste and smell, low testosterone.
Iron
Oxygen transport via haemoglobin, energy production, immune function, cognitive performance.
Red meat provides haem iron which absorbs best. Vitamin C dramatically improves non-haem iron absorption from plant sources.
Deficiency: anaemia, fatigue, cognitive impairment, cold intolerance. Most common deficiency globally.
Copper
Iron metabolism, connective tissue formation, nerve function, antioxidant enzyme (ceruloplasmin) production.
Liver, shellfish, nuts, seeds, dark chocolate. Zinc and copper compete — excess zinc supplementation depletes copper.
Deficiency: anaemia despite adequate iron, weakened connective tissue, neurological issues over time.
Iodine
Thyroid hormone production — regulates metabolism, body temperature, energy, growth, and development.
Seaweed, seafood, iodised salt. Inland populations historically deficient. Supplement if seafood intake is consistently low.
Deficiency: hypothyroidism, goitre, fatigue, weight gain, cognitive impairment in development.
Chromium
Insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism. Largely absent from modern processed diets.
Broccoli, whole grains, liver, brewer's yeast. Picolinate form for supplementation. One of Wallach's key trace minerals.
Deficiency: insulin resistance, blood sugar dysregulation, increased diabetes risk.
Manganese
Bone formation, wound healing, superoxide dismutase antioxidant enzyme, carbohydrate and fat metabolism.
Whole grains, legumes, nuts, leafy vegetables, tea. Obtained through a varied whole-food diet.
Deficiency: bone malformation, impaired glucose tolerance, poor wound healing.
Molybdenum
Detoxification enzyme function. Processes sulphur-containing amino acids. Essential to the body's detoxification chemistry.
Legumes, whole grains, liver. Often overlooked entirely in nutritional discussions.
Deficiency: sulphite sensitivity, rapid heart rate, neurological symptoms in severe depletion.
Vanadium
Insulin-mimicking activity, bone development. Consistently cited by Wallach across species studies.
Shellfish, mushrooms, black pepper, dill. Present in quality colloidal plant-source mineral supplements.
Deficiency: impaired glucose metabolism, cardiovascular issues in animal research.
Boron
Bone health alongside calcium and D3, joint function, hormone metabolism, cognitive performance.
Almonds, avocado, raisins, prunes, legumes. Soil depletion has significantly reduced its presence in modern food.
Deficiency: osteoporosis, arthritis, impaired hormone regulation, reduced cognitive function.
Silicon
Connective tissue integrity — collagen, bone, cartilage, hair, skin, nails. Structurally critical but rarely discussed.
Whole grains, oats, barley, horsetail herb. Present in quality colloidal mineral supplements.
Deficiency: brittle bones, poor skin elasticity, hair loss, weak connective tissue.
Sulphur
Protein structure, detoxification via glutathione production, joint health via MSM, skin and nail integrity.
Eggs, cruciferous vegetables — broccoli, cabbage, garlic, onion. MSM is a well-tolerated supplemental form.
Deficiency: joint pain, poor skin and hair quality, impaired detoxification, reduced glutathione levels.
Phosphorus
Bone and tooth formation alongside calcium. Energy production (ATP). Cell membrane structure. DNA synthesis.
Meat, fish, eggs, dairy, legumes. Rarely deficient in those eating adequate protein. Balance with calcium matters.
Deficiency: bone pain, weakness, poor appetite. Excess common in processed food depletes calcium.
The 60 minerals include the 16 above plus a further 44 trace minerals — cobalt, fluoride, germanium, nickel, rubidium, strontium, tin, and others — present in micro-quantities but performing specific enzymatic roles. The complete list is found in Wallach's work and in plant-source colloidal mineral supplements. The body uses what it is given. Give it the full spectrum and it has the tools to do its work.
The 16 essential vitamins divide into fat-soluble (A, D, E, K — stored in body fat) and water-soluble (C and the eight B vitamins — not stored, replenished regularly). The form of each vitamin matters as much as the dose — this is where the gap between cheap multivitamins and effective supplementation lives.

The 12 essential amino acids cannot be synthesised by the body in adequate quantities — they must come from food. They are the structural units of every protein: muscle, enzyme, hormone, antibody, neurotransmitter precursor. A diet that delivers all 12 consistently is built around complete protein sources.

Complete protein sources
Eggs — biological value 100. All 12 essential amino acids in near-perfect ratios. The gold standard. Every other protein source is measured against it.
Meat and poultry — complete. High leucine content makes these particularly effective for muscle protein synthesis.
Fish and shellfish — complete, and high in taurine, which is conditionally essential under high stress and training loads.
Dairy — complete. Whey absorbs fast (good post-training). Casein absorbs slowly (cottage cheese before sleep delivers overnight amino acid availability).
Quinoa — one of very few complete plant proteins. Combine with legumes for reinforced coverage.
Rice and lentils together — traditional cultures solved complete amino acid coverage centuries before science named the mechanism.
Glycine — not among the strict 12 but conditionally essential. Bone broth is the richest source. Supports sleep, gut lining, and collagen synthesis.
The three essential fatty acids are among the most impactful nutrients in the entire list — and among the most imbalanced in the modern diet. The ratio of omega-6 to omega-3 in industrialised eating is estimated at 15:1 to 20:1. The evolutionarily appropriate ratio is believed to be closer to 4:1. Chronic excess of omega-6 relative to omega-3 drives systemic inflammation — the common thread in virtually every modern degenerative disease.
The three essential fatty acids
ALA (Alpha-linolenic acid) — omega-3, plant source. Found in flaxseed, chia, walnuts. Converts to EPA and DHA in the body but conversion rate is poor — typically 5–10%. Do not rely on ALA as your primary omega-3 source.
EPA (Eicosapentaenoic acid) — omega-3, marine source. Anti-inflammatory. Cardiovascular health, mood regulation, joint protection. Oily fish and algae oil are the direct sources — this is where primary omega-3 intake should come from.
DHA (Docosahexaenoic acid) — omega-3, marine source. Brain structure — 60% of the brain's fat is DHA. Vision, nerve function, foetal brain development. Oily fish and algae oil. Fish get their DHA from algae in the first place.
The omega-6 imbalance and the correction
Linoleic acid (LA) — the dietary omega-6. Found in vegetable oils, seeds, nuts. Not harmful in moderation. The problem is overconsumption by an enormous margin through industrial seed oils used in almost all processed food.
What excess omega-6 does — the same enzymes that convert ALA to EPA and DHA are hijacked by excess omega-6. Omega-3 conversion drops. Inflammatory signalling rises. The balance is the issue, not either fatty acid in isolation.
The practical correction — reduce industrial seed oils (vegetable, sunflower, rapeseed). Increase oily fish to three or more portions per week. Supplement with fish oil or algae oil. The ratio improves remarkably quickly when addressed deliberately and consistently.
On saturated fat — not among the 3 essential fatty acids, but worth stating: the demonisation of saturated fat led directly to its replacement with vegetable oil — which is categorically worse for the omega-6 to omega-3 ratio and for oxidative stability at cooking temperatures.
Common Folly — The Mighty 90
Taking a cheap multivitamin and assuming all 90 essentials are covered.
A standard supermarket multivitamin contains perhaps 20 of the 90, in synthetic forms with variable bioavailability, at doses below therapeutic significance. Know what you are taking and why.
Assuming that eating well makes supplementation unnecessary in the modern era.
In depleted soils, with long supply chains and food grown for yield rather than nutrient density — even an excellent diet cannot reliably deliver all 60 minerals. The McCance and Widdowson data is published government data, not alternative health opinion.
Choosing supplement forms without understanding bioavailability.
Magnesium oxide has roughly 4% absorption. Magnesium glycinate absorbs at roughly 80%. The form is not a minor detail — it determines whether the supplement does anything at all.

CIRCADIAN BIOLOGY
The body's internal clock in full — light, hormones, the Sun, the Moon, and the 24-hour cascade
In 2017, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded for the discovery of the molecular mechanisms governing circadian clocks. The finding: every cell in the body contains its own timekeeping mechanism, and these clocks are synchronised by light, feeding time, temperature, and social cues. This confirmed what traditional peoples, farmers, and anyone who has experienced severe jet lag already knew intuitively — the body has a preferred schedule, and disrupting it carries a measurable and cumulative price.
The circadian rhythm is not merely about sleep and waking. It governs the timing of hormone release, immune function, metabolic rate, body temperature, cognitive performance, muscle repair, cardiovascular function, and gut motility. Every system in the body has a peak performance window. Those windows are set by the clock. Ignore the clock and you are working against your own biology with every decision you make.

"Every cell in your body has a clock. When those clocks fall out of sync, the body ages faster, repairs slower, and performs worse at everything it tries to do."

Light is the master signal. The suprachiasmatic nucleus — roughly 20,000 neurons in the hypothalamus — receives light signals directly from the retina and synchronises every other clock in the body. Morning light exposure is not a lifestyle preference. It is the act of setting the biological clock for the entire day. Bright outdoor light within the first hour of waking — even on an overcast day — is the single most powerful circadian anchor available. It costs nothing. It takes five minutes. It changes everything downstream.
The Sun and the Moon. Before artificial light, the human circadian rhythm was governed entirely by the solar cycle — dawn cortisol, midday peak, evening wind-down, overnight repair. Moonlight — far dimmer than any artificial source and carrying insufficient blue-spectrum energy to suppress melatonin — was the only nocturnal light. The body still expects this. The cells that read light signals do not know what century they are in. They respond to the wavelength they receive. Give them the right signal at the right time and every downstream system functions with less resistance. The majesty of the Sun and the Moon is not merely poetic — it is biological.
The hormone cascade — 24 hours
Cortisol — peaks at dawn, falls through the day. Drives alertness, anti-inflammation, and blood sugar regulation. Chronically elevated cortisol from sleep deprivation degrades muscle, impairs immunity, and disrupts every downstream hormone in the cascade.
Testosterone — peaks in the early morning. Declines through the day. One week of sleep restriction reduces testosterone by 15–25% in healthy young men — equivalent in hormonal terms to ageing a decade.
Growth hormone — released in pulses during deep sleep, primarily in the first two cycles before midnight. The primary driver of tissue repair. Training without adequate sleep is training without the recovery signal.
Melatonin — rises after dark, suppressed by blue-spectrum light. The timing of melatonin onset determines sleep architecture and depth. Phones, screens, and LED lighting suppress it reliably and measurably — even brief evening exposure is enough to delay onset by an hour.
Insulin — sensitivity varies dramatically by time of day. The same meal at 8am has a significantly different metabolic effect than the same meal at 8pm. Late eating is not merely a caloric question — it is a timing question.
What disrupts the clock
Artificial light after dark — blue-spectrum light from screens signals the SCN that it is still daytime. Melatonin is suppressed. Sleep onset is delayed. Sleep depth is reduced. Blue-light filtering after sunset is not a preference — it is maintenance of the body's most fundamental repair window.
Irregular sleep timing — different sleep and wake times on different days creates social jet lag. The clock cannot anchor. Every system dependent on timed hormone release is disrupted.
Eating late — the gut has its own circadian rhythm. Eating in the two to three hours before sleep forces digestive activity into the repair window. Insulin and melatonin actively oppose each other at the molecular level — they cannot both peak simultaneously without consequence.
No morning light exposure — waking in a dark room and spending the morning indoors leaves the clock unset. By midday, the entire hormone cascade is running without its anchor. Step outside within the first hour of waking — even briefly, even in overcast weather.
Shift work — consistently misaligning the social clock with the biological clock is associated with significantly elevated risk of metabolic disorder, cardiovascular disease, depression, and certain cancers. If unavoidable, deliberate light management and consistent meal timing can partially mitigate the damage.
Common Folly — Circadian Biology
Staying up late regularly and framing it as being a "night owl" — treating a disrupted rhythm as a personality trait.
True night owls exist but are far rarer than claimed. Most late chronotypes are trained not born — shaped by years of artificial light and irregular schedules. The clock is adjustable. Consistent morning light exposure shifts it reliably over weeks.
Treating sleep as the variable to sacrifice when life gets busy — planning to "catch up at the weekend."
Sleep debt does not clear cleanly on weekends. Cognitive and physical performance deficits from chronic short sleep accumulate and persist. Weekend recovery sleep partially addresses acute debt but does not restore the hormonal cascade of consistent, well-timed sleep.
Training intensely late at night and wondering why sleep quality is poor.
Intense exercise raises core body temperature, cortisol, and adrenaline — all of which actively oppose melatonin onset. The body needs 2–4 hours to return to sleep-ready temperature. Train before 7pm where possible.
Using caffeine to override the afternoon dip rather than working with it.
Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors — it does not eliminate adenosine, merely delays its signal. When caffeine clears, accumulated adenosine hits at once. A 20-minute nap before 3pm is physiologically superior. Caffeine after 2pm measurably degrades sleep quality in most people — even those who believe they are unaffected.

DETOXIFICATION
The liver, the lymph, the pathways — and the crucial warning about mobilising without clearing
Detoxification is among the most misunderstood concepts in health. Captured by the wellness industry and reduced to green juices and three-day cleanses — which is not merely ineffective but occasionally counterproductive — genuine detoxification is a continuous biological process the body conducts around the clock. The liver, kidneys, lymphatic system, skin, and lungs are running detoxification processes at this moment, as they always are. Understanding how they work — and what supports or undermines them — is the honest version of this conversation.
The modern body faces an environmental burden entirely absent from pre-industrial human experience: heavy metals from industry and dental materials, pesticide residues in food and water, pharmaceutical compounds in water supplies, plasticisers from packaging, air pollutants, endocrine-disrupting chemicals in everyday products. The body's detoxification systems evolved for a different world. They are not broken — they are overwhelmed. Supporting them is not a fringe idea. It is a rational response to an unprecedented situation.

"The dose makes the poison — but the body's ability to clear the dose makes the difference between burden and damage."

Paracelsus — updated for the present century
500+
Liver functions
The liver synthesises proteins, metabolises hormones, produces bile, stores glycogen, and runs two full phases of detoxification chemistry. It is not merely a filter.
3
Phases of liver detox
Phase I activates toxins — temporarily making them more reactive. Phase II conjugates them for removal. Phase III exports them. All three must work or toxins recirculate.
!
The crucial warning
Mobilising toxins from storage without clear elimination pathways allows them to recirculate and deposit elsewhere — potentially causing more damage than leaving them stored.
The most important thing to understand about detoxification: toxins mobilised from tissue storage — fat cells, bone, organs — must have a clear exit route. If the liver is congested, the lymph is sluggish, bowels are not moving daily, and hydration is inadequate, mobilised toxins simply redistribute to other tissues. Cilantro mobilises heavy metals effectively. Without simultaneously supporting elimination — good hydration, active lymph, daily bowel movement, liver support, chlorella to bind metals in the gut — those metals can redeposit. Mobilise and clear. Always in that order. Always simultaneously.
Phase I — Liver activation
Cytochrome P450 enzymes convert fat-soluble toxins into water-soluble intermediates — temporarily making them more chemically reactive. If Phase II cannot keep pace, these intermediates accumulate and cause damage.
Supports Phase I — B vitamins (B2, B3, B6, B12, folate), vitamin C, magnesium, iron, cruciferous vegetables via indole-3-carbinol, adequate dietary protein.
Inhibits Phase I — grapefruit (naringenin), certain medications, alcohol, nutritional deficiency. This is why grapefruit interacts with so many pharmaceuticals — it inhibits the very enzyme that metabolises them.
Phase I without Phase II — the dangerous scenario. Aggressive detox protocols that activate Phase I without supporting Phase II create a backlog of reactive intermediates. This is how poorly designed detox programmes cause harm.
Phase II — Conjugation pathways
Six conjugation pathways bind Phase I intermediates to carrier molecules — making them water-soluble and export-ready. The six pathways: glutathione conjugation, sulphation, glucuronidation, methylation, acetylation, amino acid conjugation.
Glutathione conjugation — the master pathway. Requires cysteine (eggs, cruciferous vegetables), glycine (bone broth), and glutamate. NAC directly supports glutathione production and is the most accessible Phase II support available.
Sulphation — requires sulphur amino acids (methionine, cysteine) and adequate molybdenum. Relevant to oestrogen metabolism, neurotransmitter clearance, and certain food chemical processing.
Methylation — requires methyl donors: folate (methylfolate form), B12 (methylcobalamin), B6, betaine. Genetic variants (MTHFR) can impair this pathway significantly — a reason why active-form vitamins matter.
Key detox supports — herbal and nutritional
Milk thistle (silymarin) — most researched liver-protective herb. Protects liver cells from toxic damage and supports cellular regeneration. Decades of clinical evidence across multiple populations and conditions.
Dandelion root — supports bile production and liver function. Mildly diuretic supporting kidney elimination. Often overlooked because it grows freely in gardens and costs nothing.
NAC (N-acetyl cysteine) — directly precedes glutathione synthesis. Protects liver from oxidative damage. Used clinically for paracetamol overdose — which tells you precisely how potent its liver-protective capacity is.
CoQ10 (Coenzyme Q10) — essential to mitochondrial energy production and a powerful antioxidant protecting the liver and heart during detoxification. Statin drugs deplete CoQ10 dramatically — a fact insufficiently communicated to those prescribed them.
Alpha lipoic acid — both water and fat soluble. Regenerates glutathione, vitamin C, and vitamin E. Chelates certain heavy metals including mercury and arsenic. An unusually versatile antioxidant.
Heavy metal clearance — in the correct order
Step 1 — Open elimination pathways first. Daily bowel movement through fibre and hydration. Good kidney function through water and potassium. Active lymph through movement and dry brushing. Regular sweating.
Step 2 — Bind metals in the gut. Chlorella — demonstrated binding capacity for mercury, lead, and cadmium in the intestine. Zeolite (clinoptilolite). Modified citrus pectin. Bind before recirculation occurs.
Step 3 — Mobilise from tissues. Cilantro and coriander mobilise heavy metals from tissues — only once elimination pathways are open and binding agents are already in place. Never mobilise without the exit route prepared.
Step 4 — Hydration and skin clearance. Clean water with a pinch of sea salt per litre for electrolyte balance. Wash sweat from skin promptly after exercise or sauna — do not allow excreted compounds to be reabsorbed through the skin surface.
Step 5 — Restore minerals. Chelation protocols remove essential minerals alongside toxic ones. Replenish with mineral-rich food and quality supplementation throughout the entire protocol.
The lymphatic system has no pump of its own. Unlike the cardiovascular system driven by the heart, lymph moves only through muscle contraction, breathing, and deliberate stimulation. A sedentary lifestyle means sluggish lymph — accumulated cellular waste, compromised immunity, and reduced toxin clearance. Movement is not optional for lymphatic health. Walking, jumping, rebounding on a mini-trampoline, and deep diaphragmatic breathing move more lymph than any supplement. The simplest lymphatic protocol in existence: move daily, breathe deeply, stay hydrated.
Common Folly — Detoxification
Beginning an aggressive detox protocol without first ensuring elimination pathways are open and functioning.
Mobilise and clear — always simultaneously. A congested liver, sluggish bowel, and inadequate hydration mean mobilised toxins have nowhere to go. Start with the basics before adding mobilising agents of any kind.
Confusing a "detox tea" containing senna or laxative herbs with genuine detoxification.
These products produce bowel movement, creating a sensation of cleansing. They do not touch Phase I or Phase II liver chemistry, the lymphatic system, or tissue-stored toxins. They are laxatives. Call them that honestly.
Dismissing detoxification entirely as pseudoscience because the wellness industry has sensationalised it.
Phase I and Phase II liver biotransformation is biochemistry taught in medical schools — not alternative medicine. The question is not whether the body detoxifies but whether it is doing so adequately under an unprecedented toxic burden. Supporting an overloaded system is rational.
Allowing sweat to dry on the skin after exercise or sauna without washing it off.
Sweat carries excreted compounds to the skin surface. Reabsorption through the skin is modest but real and cumulative. Shower after sweating. Do not let it sit and dry.

POLYPHENOLS & BIOFLAVONOIDS
The plant compounds the modern diet overlooks — and the body quietly misses
Polyphenols are a large family of naturally occurring plant compounds — more than 8,000 identified so far — that act as the plant's own defence system against UV radiation, pathogens, and oxidative stress. When we eat those plants, we absorb these compounds and they perform a remarkable range of protective functions in the human body: anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-carcinogenic, cardioprotective, neuroprotective, and microbiome-supporting. Bioflavonoids are a major sub-family of polyphenols — the ones responsible for much of the colour in fruits and vegetables.
The instruction to eat a rainbow of colours is not aesthetic advice. It is a shorthand for polyphenol diversity. The modern diet, even when it includes vegetables, tends toward a narrow colour spectrum: pale lettuce, white potato, beige grain. The polyphenol density of industrially grown produce is also measurably lower than traditionally grown varieties. Colour intensity in fruit and vegetables is a direct indicator of polyphenol concentration. The deeper the colour, the more protective compounds are present.

"Let food be thy medicine. The colour is the medicine."

Hippocrates — updated for the molecular era
Quercetin
Flavonol — yellow/white pigment
Anti-inflammatory, antihistamine, antiviral. Inhibits mast cell degranulation reducing allergic response. Cardioprotective. Senolytic activity — selectively clears damaged senescent cells that accumulate with ageing.
Red onions, capers, apples with skin, broccoli, kale, berries
Resveratrol
Stilbenoid — red/purple pigment
Activates sirtuins — longevity-associated genes. Antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, cardioprotective. Mimics certain cellular effects of caloric restriction. One of the most studied longevity compounds currently in research.
Red grapes and their skin, blueberries, mulberries, dark chocolate, peanuts
Curcumin
Curcuminoid — deep yellow/orange pigment
Potent anti-inflammatory comparable to some NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) without the side effects. Liver support, raises BDNF for brain health, joint protection. Poor bioavailability alone — requires black pepper (piperine) and fat for meaningful absorption.
Turmeric root and powder. Always combine with black pepper and a fat source for absorption.
EGCG
Catechin — green tea compound
Epigallocatechin gallate — the primary active compound in green tea. Antioxidant, anti-carcinogenic, supports fat metabolism, neuroprotective, antimicrobial. One of the most extensively researched polyphenols in existence.
Green tea — matcha has highest concentration. White tea. Black tea to lesser extent.
Anthocyanins
Flavonoid — red/blue/purple pigment
Antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, cardioprotective, neuroprotective. Crosses the blood-brain barrier — directly supports cognitive function and memory consolidation. The compounds responsible for making blueberries blue and red cabbage purple.
Blueberries, blackberries, cherries, red cabbage, black beans, elderberries, red onion
Sulforaphane
Isothiocyanate — not a pigment
Activates Nrf2 — the master switch for the body's antioxidant and detoxification gene expression. Anti-carcinogenic, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective. Among the most potent food-derived protective compounds identified by research.
Broccoli sprouts (50–100x more than mature broccoli). Broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage. Chop and leave 40 min before cooking to allow sulforaphane formation.
Fisetin
Flavonol — orange/red pigment
Potent senolytic — clears damaged senescent cells. Neuroprotective. Extends lifespan in animal models more consistently than most other studied compounds. Significantly under-discussed relative to its research profile and potential.
Strawberries (highest known dietary source), apples, persimmon, lotus root, cucumber skin
Apigenin
Flavone — pale yellow pigment
Anti-inflammatory, anxiolytic via GABA receptor binding. Inhibits CD38 — an enzyme that depletes NAD+, the molecule central to cellular energy and repair. Research interest growing rapidly in the longevity space.
Chamomile tea (very high concentration), parsley, celery, artichoke, oregano
Luteolin
Flavone — yellow pigment
Anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective. Inhibits inflammatory cytokines. Stabilises mast cells — particularly relevant for those with inflammatory or allergic conditions. Often found alongside apigenin in the same food sources.
Celery, parsley, artichoke, thyme, chamomile tea, red and green peppers
The gut microbiome connection. Many polyphenols are not absorbed in the small intestine but travel to the large intestine where gut bacteria transform them into bioactive metabolites — often more potent than the original polyphenol compound. This means gut microbiome diversity directly determines how much benefit a polyphenol-rich diet actually delivers to the body. A diverse microbiome, fed by a diverse plant diet, creates a virtuous cycle: more polyphenols feed more diverse bacteria, which produce more protective metabolites, which support the gut lining, which protects the microbiome. The starting point is simply eating a wider variety of plants than you currently do — every week, consistently.
Practical polyphenol strategy
Eat by colour — every week include red, orange, yellow, green, blue/purple, and white plant foods. Each colour band represents different polyphenol families with different protective mechanisms and targets in the body.
Prioritise intensity — deeper colours indicate higher polyphenol density. Wild blueberries beat cultivated. Dark leafy greens beat iceberg. A deep red tomato beats a pale one every time.
Broccoli sprouts weekly — the most potent sulforaphane source available without specialised supplementation. Three to four days from seed in a jar on any windowsill. Eat raw or very lightly steamed to preserve the compound.
Green tea daily — quality green tea delivers EGCG and L-theanine, which moderates caffeine's edge and supports calm focus. Matcha delivers significantly higher concentration than brewed leaf tea of the same weight.
Seasonal and local where possible — polyphenol content peaks at harvest and declines with time, distance, and cold-storage duration. Seasonal produce eaten close to its source has measurably higher concentrations than the same food shipped internationally out of season.
Bioflavonoids — the honourable mention
Bioflavonoids are a specific and numerous sub-group of polyphenols. They were originally classified as "Vitamin P" in the 1930s before the classification was revised. The name was dropped; the importance was not.
Vitamin C synergy — bioflavonoids dramatically enhance vitamin C absorption and stability in the body. Whole citrus fruit delivers bioflavonoids alongside vitamin C in the white pith. Ascorbic acid supplement alone does not replicate this synergy.
Rutin — found in buckwheat, asparagus, and apple skin. Strengthens capillary walls, reduces inflammation, supports circulation. Particularly relevant for those with varicose veins, easy bruising, or poor peripheral circulation.
Hesperidin — concentrated in citrus pith and peel. Anti-inflammatory, supports lymphatic function, neuroprotective. Mostly discarded when people juice citrus or eat only the flesh, removing the most medicinally valuable part of the fruit.
The pith is the medicine — the white spongy layer between citrus skin and flesh that most people remove is where the highest bioflavonoid concentration resides. Eat the whole fruit. The inconvenience is trivial. The benefit is not.
Common Folly — Polyphenols & Bioflavonoids
Juicing fruit and discarding the fibre and pith to "get the nutrients."
The juice delivers sugar and some vitamins. The fibre slows sugar absorption. The pith delivers bioflavonoids. The pulp feeds the microbiome. Juicing discards most of the therapeutic value and leaves you with essentially a sweet drink.
Relying on a single "superfood" rather than dietary diversity.
No single food delivers the full polyphenol spectrum. Diversity of plant intake is the single strongest dietary predictor of microbiome health — and microbiome health determines how much benefit polyphenols actually deliver to the body.
Boiling broccoli at high heat and expecting significant sulforaphane benefit.
Sulforaphane forms when myrosinase enzyme contacts glucoraphanin. Myrosinase is destroyed above 60 degrees celsius. Chop broccoli and leave it 40 minutes before cooking — sulforaphane forms during this window and is then heat-stable. Or eat it raw. Or grow the sprouts, which have 50–100 times more to begin with.

MIND WORKS

Deliberate mental states — Intention, Expectation, Attention — measurably alter neurochemistry and, through neurochemistry, alter physical performance and recovery. The mind is not the passenger in this journey. It is the driver.

The neuroscience of habit
Habit formation — every repeated behaviour creates and reinforces a neural pathway. The more frequently a pathway is used, the more myelin — the insulating sheath that speeds neural transmission — is laid down around it. Habits are literally wired in at the cellular level. This works in both directions: helpful and harmful habits are equally structural and equally persistent.
The habit loop — cue, routine, reward. Identifying the cue that triggers an unwanted routine and replacing only the routine (keeping the cue and the reward intact) is far more effective than attempting elimination through willpower alone. The architecture of the loop is the leverage point.
Neuroplasticity — the brain rewires throughout life, not only in childhood. New pathways form in response to new experience and deliberate practice. The formation of beneficial habits is a literal act of self-reconstruction at the neural level. The body follows the brain's map.
The 66-day figure — habit automaticity typically develops over 66 days of consistent practice, not the commonly cited 21. Foundation Level's 90-day structure accounts for this with room to establish the habit before the programme ends.
Stress and the body
Acute stress is useful — the fight-or-flight response sharpens focus, mobilises energy, and heightens physical performance in the short term. The body designed for a world of acute stressors handles this beautifully and efficiently.
Chronic stress is corrosive — cortisol elevated continuously suppresses immune function, degrades muscle protein, impairs memory consolidation, disrupts sleep architecture, and promotes visceral fat accumulation. The body was not designed for this. Modern life frequently provides it without pause.
The HPA axis — the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis is the hormonal stress pathway. Chronic activation depletes adrenal reserves and disrupts the cortisol awakening response, creating the flat exhausted state commonly described as burnout or adrenal fatigue.
Recovery is not passive — the parasympathetic nervous system (rest and digest) must be deliberately activated to counteract chronic sympathetic (fight or flight) dominance. Slow breathing, nature contact, deliberate stillness, and adequate sleep all activate the parasympathetic response. These are not luxuries. They are maintenance for the system that runs everything else.

Intention shapes what you expect. Expectation determines where your Attention goes. Where Attention goes, results follow — eventually, inevitably, like a ball set rolling until it carries the momentum of a juggernaut. The mind sets the direction. The body follows it — always.

Common Folly — Mind Works
Believing that willpower is a fixed character trait — something you either have or simply don't.
Willpower is a resource that depletes with use and restores with rest — it behaves more like a muscle than a personality trait. It is also largely unnecessary when habits are well-established. The goal of the first 90 days is to reduce reliance on willpower by building automaticity. Design the environment. The character follows.
Treating mental and emotional health as entirely separate from physical health.
The gut-brain axis alone — bidirectional communication between the enteric nervous system and the central nervous system — should dissolve this distinction. 90% of serotonin is produced in the gut. Gut health is mood health. Physical health is mental health. The division is administrative, not biological.
Dismissing meditation and breathwork as spiritual practices with no physiological relevance.
Controlled breathing directly activates the vagus nerve and the parasympathetic nervous system. Heart rate variability, cortisol levels, and blood pressure all respond measurably and reliably to deliberate breath practice. The mechanism is physiology. The results are physical. Call it what it is and use it accordingly.

BREAKING THROUGH PLATEAU
Why it comes to everyone — and why it separates those who become from those who almost did
The plateau will come. Not might — will. It is not a sign that the programme is broken. It is not a sign that you are broken. It is a sign that the body has done precisely what it was asked to do: it has adapted to the current stimulus. The ball is rolling. The question now is what you do with the momentum.
Most people experience the plateau as failure. Progress was happening — measurably, visibly, encouragingly — and then it stopped. The exercises that used to be hard became merely difficult, then routine, then easy. The body stopped changing. The common interpretation is that the approach has stopped working. The honest interpretation is that the approach has finished working at this level — and that the next level is now accessible precisely because this one has been fully mastered.

"The impediment to action advances action. What stands in the way becomes the way."

Marcus Aurelius — Meditations · Written nearly 2,000 years before sports science confirmed it
What the plateau actually is
The body adapts to a training stimulus through a process called supercompensation. Apply a stress. The body is disrupted. It recovers. In recovering it overshoots the previous baseline — becoming slightly more capable than before. Apply the same stress again. The body recovers again, overshoots again. Progress accumulates. But if the same stress is applied repeatedly without variation, the body adapts to that specific stimulus and stops needing to overshoot. The stimulus has become the new baseline. This is the plateau — not failure, but successful adaptation demanding a new and greater challenge.
The body is not refusing to grow. It is telling you it has grown as far as this particular stimulus will take it. The plateau is the invitation to the next level. It always has been.
4–6
Weeks to plateau onset
Most training adaptations plateau within 4–6 weeks of consistent identical stimulus. The body is efficient — it adapts faster than most people expect or plan for.
3
Variables to change
Volume (sets times reps), intensity (difficulty of variation), and rest periods. Change any one and the stimulus is new. Change all three and the body has no map to work from.
≠
Plateau does not equal failure
The person who never hits a plateau has never pushed hard enough to fully adapt. The plateau is the view from the summit of one level, looking up toward the next.
Why people fall back at the plateau
Misidentification — the plateau is read as failure rather than as successful adaptation. The emotional response to "it is not working" is to stop. The correct response is to change the stimulus and continue.
Comparison to early gains — the first weeks of a programme produce rapid visible change driven largely by neural adaptations, not structural ones. When these fast early gains naturally slow, it feels like regression. It is not. Neurological adaptation is simply complete. Structural change is slower, less dramatic, and far more durable.
Boredom dressed as wisdom — "this programme is not right for me" often means "I am no longer excited by results that were exciting when the programme was new." The excitement of novelty wears off. The work continues regardless. These are entirely different things.
Fear of the next level — progressing from inverted row to pull-up, from pike push-up to handstand push-up, from assisted pistol to full pistol — requires genuinely attempting something difficult again. Some people prefer the comfort of the plateau to the discomfort of being a beginner again at the next level. This is the most honest and the most common reason people fall back into insignificance after early progress.
How to break through — On-Purpose
Progress the variation — the programme already maps this precisely. When the current variation is genuinely comfortable for all sets, the next one is not optional. It is the programme doing its job.
Change the tempo — a 3-second controlled negative turns a familiar exercise into a new stimulus immediately. A 5-second negative harder still. A pause rep at the most difficult point of the movement hardest of all. The movement is the same. The time under tension is not.
Reduce rest intervals — the same exercise, the same reps, but 45 seconds rest instead of 90. The cardiovascular and metabolic demand changes significantly. The body must adapt to a genuinely different stimulus.
Introduce external load — a backpack with weight, a weighted vest. Even modest load on a bodyweight exercise is a meaningfully new stimulus the body has not encountered before.
Deload and return — sometimes the plateau is not a stimulus problem but a recovery deficit. The programme builds in three deload weeks precisely for this reason. A deliberate deload followed by a return to full training frequently breaks a plateau that adding more training was making progressively worse.
Address sleep and nutrition first — a training plateau that coincides with poor sleep or inadequate protein is a recovery problem, not a programming problem. No variation of stimulus repairs insufficient recovery. Address it in the correct order.
The mental dimension of plateau
The physical plateau and the psychological plateau are the same event experienced in two different languages. Physically, the stimulus is insufficient to drive further adaptation. Mentally, the Intention has softened — the original urgency has faded into familiarity. The Expectation has drifted — the initial belief that change was coming has been partially replaced by the assumption that change has finished. The Attention has become mechanical — the session is being completed rather than genuinely inhabited. Revisiting Intention at the plateau is not a motivational exercise. It is a technical one. What do you actually want now that the early gains have been made? What does the next level of capability mean to you? Where is your Attention during a working set — on the muscle contracting, on the count, or on what you are having for dinner? The answers to these questions are precisely where the breakthrough lives, waiting.
Reset the Intention. Raise the Expectation. Bring the Attention fully back into the room. Then change the stimulus. In that order. Every time.
There is a particular kind of person who pushes through the plateau. They are not more gifted. They are not more disciplined in any innate sense. They have simply developed — through experience or through being told early enough — the habit of interpreting the plateau correctly. As signal rather than verdict. They know that the plateau is precisely the place where almost everyone turns back toward comfort and familiarity. And that knowledge makes the path through it visible when it arrives. You are reading this because you now know. That changes what the plateau means when it comes — and it will come.
Common Folly — Breaking Through Plateau
Changing the programme entirely when the current one stops producing obvious visible results.
Programme-hopping is the most reliable way to perpetually remain a beginner. The discomfort of the plateau is precisely where adaptation happens. Stay with the system long enough to outgrow it deliberately — then progress within it, not away from it.
Interpreting a training plateau as evidence that the body does not respond to this kind of exercise.
Every body responds to an appropriate stimulus. The plateau is evidence that the current stimulus has been fully mastered. That is success. Change the stimulus and progress continues. The body's capacity is not the limit. The stimulus is.
Adding more volume to break a plateau that is actually being caused by a recovery deficit.
More training on an under-recovered body produces injury and regression — not adaptation. If sleep is poor, protein is insufficient, and stress is chronically elevated, adding sessions makes the plateau worse with each session. Address recovery first. Always in that order.
