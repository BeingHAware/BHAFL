01
Movement Family One
PUSH
Chest, shoulders, and triceps working to press the body away from a surface — or press a surface away from the body

Push movements train the anterior chain — the pressing muscles of the upper body. In calisthenics, push exercises split into horizontal push (push-up and its progressions, training the chest and triceps) and vertical push (pike push-up, dip, and handstand progressions, training the shoulders). Both are essential. Neglect one and the shoulder develops an imbalance that will eventually make itself known.

Push · Horizontal · Exercise 01 of 05
PUSH-UP
The foundation of all horizontal pressing — and the exercise most people have never done correctly
Horizontal PushNo equipmentBeginner → Advanced
Frame 1 — Starting Position
Hands slightly wider than shoulder width, fingers pointing forward, body in a straight plank line from head to heel. This is the position you maintain throughout.
Muscles Working
Primary
Pectoralis major — chest pressing force. Triceps brachii — elbow extension at lockout.
Secondary
Anterior deltoid — front shoulder assistance. Serratus anterior — scapular stability and protraction.
Stabilising
Core — entire anterior chain maintaining the plank. Glutes — preventing hip sag. Lats — shoulder stability.
Step by Step
1
Set the hands
Place hands slightly wider than shoulders, fingers spread, weight distributed through the whole palm — not just the heel of the hand.
2
Build the plank
Squeeze the glutes, brace the core, pull the ribcage down. Body is one rigid unit from ears to heels. No sagging hips, no raised backside.
Feel it: everything tight before you move a single inch
3
Lower with control
Elbows track at 45° from the body — not flared wide, not tucked in tight. Lower until the chest touches the floor. Two-second descent.
Feel it: chest, not chin, reaches the floor first
4
Press to full lockout
Push the floor away explosively. Fully extend the elbows at the top. The plank must remain intact throughout — do not let the hips sag as you press.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Push-Up
Elbows flaring wide — making a T-shape with the arms.
Elbows at 45° from the body throughout. Think of making an arrow shape, not a T. This protects the shoulder joint and increases pectoral recruitment.
Hips sagging toward the floor — losing the plank.
Squeeze the glutes and brace the core before every rep. If the hips sag, the push-up has become a lumbar extension exercise. Elevate the hands to reduce the load until the plank can be maintained.
Partial range — chin reaching but chest not touching.
Full range means chest to floor, arms to lockout. Partial reps build partial strength. If the full range is not yet available, elevate the hands until it is.
Progressions
Wall / Incline push-up
Hands elevated, reducing load. Builds the pattern with correct elbows before bodyweight is demanded.
Beginner
Full push-up ← here
Bodyweight on the floor. Standard. Target 3×15 before progressing.
Intermediate
Archer push-up
One arm extends wide as the other bends. Asymmetric load approaching the one-arm.
Advanced
One-arm push-up
Single arm pressing the full bodyweight. The pinnacle of horizontal push strength.
Advanced
Fillers when not yet available
Wall push-upKnee push-upIncline push-upNegative push-up
Push · Vertical · Exercise 02 of 05
PIKE PUSH-UP
The gateway to overhead pressing strength — and the direct precursor to the handstand push-up
Vertical PushNo equipmentIntermediate
Frame 1 — Starting Position
Downward dog position — hips high, hands shoulder-width, heels reaching toward the floor. The higher the hips, the greater the shoulder demand. This is your anchor point.
Muscles Working
Primary
Anterior deltoid — front shoulder pressing. Triceps brachii — elbow extension throughout.
Secondary
Upper trapezius — stabilising the shoulder girdle overhead. Serratus anterior — scapular control.
Step by Step
1
Set the pike
Downward dog, hips as high as possible, forming an inverted V. The more acute the angle, the more this becomes a vertical press.
2
Lower the crown toward the floor
Bend the elbows and lower the top of the head toward the ground between your hands. The head travels forward as it descends.
Feel it: loading in the front shoulders, not the chest
3
Press back to the pike
Push the floor away and return to the inverted V. Hips stay high throughout. If the hips drop toward horizontal, this becomes a push-up.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Pike Push-Up
Hips dropping during the movement — converting it into a push-up.
The hips stay elevated throughout. If they drift down, the front shoulder loading disappears. Think of the hips as a fixed point — only the head and arms move.
Head not reaching the floor — treating it as a stretch rather than a press.
Full range means the crown of the head reaches the floor between the hands. This is a strength exercise, not a yoga pose. If the range isn't there yet, elevate the feet to increase the angle.
Progressions
Pike push-up on flat
Standard version on the floor. Target 3×10 before elevating the feet.
Intermediate
Elevated feet pike ← here
Feet on a chair or box increases the angle and the shoulder demand significantly.
Advanced
Handstand push-up
Full vertical press against the wall. The completed form of this movement pattern.
Advanced
Fillers when not yet available
Pike holdWall shoulder tapPike negative
Push · Vertical · Exercise 03 of 05
DIP
The upper body squat — demanding full range, full control, and a shoulder that has been properly prepared
Vertical PushParallel bars or chairIntermediate
Frame 1 — Starting Position
Arms fully extended on parallel bars, body upright or slightly forward, shoulders depressed and away from the ears. This is the top position — earn every rep from here.
Muscles Working
Primary
Pectoralis major lower — greater with forward lean. Triceps brachii — greater with upright torso. Anterior deltoid — throughout.
Stabilising
Rotator cuff — shoulder joint integrity under load. Core — preventing swing.
Step by Step
1
Set the top position
Arms fully locked, shoulders depressed. Lean slightly forward for chest emphasis, stay upright for triceps emphasis.
2
Lower with control
Bend the elbows, descend until the upper arm is at least parallel to the ground. Go deeper if the shoulder allows.
Feel it: deep stretch in the chest and front shoulder at the bottom
3
Press to full lockout
Push through the palms back to full arm extension. No partial reps — the lockout is where the triceps work hardest.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Dip
Elbows flaring outward — placing destructive stress on the shoulder joint.
Elbows track behind the body throughout. Forward flare is a sign the shoulder is not yet strong enough for the load. Reduce the range until control is established.
Partial range — stopping before the upper arm reaches parallel.
Parallel is the minimum. The strength built in a partial range will not transfer to exercises that demand full range. Earn the depth over time with negative dips.
Attempting dips before the shoulder is prepared.
The dip is not a beginner exercise. The shoulder joint requires significant preparation through push-up volume, scapular work, and pike push-ups before dips are loaded on it. Rushing this is the most reliable way to acquire a shoulder impingement.
Progressions
Bench dip
Feet on floor, hands on bench behind. Lower load, useful for building the pattern.
Beginner
Parallel bar dip ← here
Full bodyweight on parallel bars. Target 3×12 before adding load.
Intermediate
Weighted dip
Belt or dumbbell adds external load. The progression for those who have outgrown bodyweight.
Advanced
Ring dip
Rings add an unstable surface demanding significantly greater stabiliser activation.
Advanced
Fillers when not yet available
Bench dipNegative dipPush-up (high volume)
Push · Horizontal · Exercise 04 of 05
ARCHER PUSH-UP
The bridge between the standard push-up and the one-arm — asymmetric load that reveals and corrects imbalances
Horizontal PushNo equipmentAdvanced
Frame 1 — Wide Starting Position
Hands placed wider than a standard push-up. Both arms extended. This wider base allows one arm to extend fully while the other bends through the full range.
Muscles Working
Primary (working side)
Pectoralis major — primary pressing force on the bent arm side. Triceps — elbow extension on the working arm.
Secondary
Extended arm — acts as a support and guide, not the primary mover. Core — preventing rotation throughout the asymmetric load.
Step by Step
1
Set a wide base
Hands wider than shoulder width. One hand will bend, one will extend. Decide which side starts as the working arm.
2
Lower toward the working arm
Shift the body laterally toward the bending arm while the opposite arm extends fully to the side, fingers pointing outward.
Feel it: the working side loads like a one-arm push-up
3
Press and alternate
Press back through the working arm. Shift to the other side for the next rep, or complete all reps on one side before switching.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Archer Push-Up
The extended arm bending and becoming a second working arm — turning it back into a standard push-up.
The extended arm should remain straight throughout, palm flat on the floor. It guides and provides minimal support — it does not press. If the arm keeps bending, widen the hand placement further.
Allowing the hips to rotate toward the working side.
The hips and shoulders stay square throughout. Rotation is the body avoiding the difficulty. Brace the core harder and slow the movement down.
Progressions
Wide push-up
Builds the wider hand position before asymmetric load is introduced.
Intermediate
Archer push-up ← here
Full asymmetric version. Target 3×8 each side.
Advanced
One-arm push-up
Full single-arm press. The archer is the final preparation for this.
Advanced
Fillers when not yet available
Wide push-upStandard push-up (high volume)Negative archer
Push · Planche Skill / Horizontal · Exercise 05 of 05
PSEUDO PLANCHE PUSH-UP
The push-up that builds toward the planche — anterior deltoid dominant, wrist intensive, technically demanding
Horizontal PushNo equipmentAdvanced
Frame 1 — Hand Position
Hands placed beside the hips with fingers pointing backward or sideways. The wrist is in extension — prepare this position gradually over weeks before loading it heavily.
Muscles Working
Primary
Anterior deltoid — the dominant mover in this position. Triceps — elbow extension. Wrist flexors — supporting the extended wrist position.
Secondary
Pectoralis minor — scapular positioning. Serratus anterior — protraction under load in the planche position.
Step by Step
1
Position the hands at the hips
Place hands beside the hips, fingers pointing backward or to the sides. Begin with a slight hand angle and progressively rotate further back as wrist strength develops.
2
Lean forward into the planche position
Shift the body forward over the hands until the shoulders are past the wrists. This transfers the load from the chest to the anterior shoulder.
Feel it: front shoulder bearing the majority of bodyweight
3
Lower and press
Keeping the forward lean, lower toward the floor and press back. Maintain the lean throughout — do not allow the hips to rise or the body to rock backward.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Pseudo Planche Push-Up
Loading the wrists in full extension without adequate preparation.
Wrist conditioning is a prerequisite. Spend several weeks with progressive wrist extension drills, push-up handle work, and knuckle push-ups before placing significant load through this position.
Insufficient forward lean — keeping the body upright and doing a standard push-up.
The forward lean is the exercise. Without the body shifted past the wrists, the anterior deltoid does not engage in the planche-specific way. Lean further forward until the front shoulder truly loads.
Progressions
Planche lean hold
Body leaned forward over the hands, arms straight. Builds the shoulder and wrist position before pressing.
Intermediate
Pseudo planche push-up ← here
Full pressing version. Target 3×8 with controlled forward lean.
Advanced
Tuck planche push-up
Hips tucked, feet off the floor. The transition into true planche work.
Advanced
Fillers when not yet available
Planche leanWrist prep drillsPush-up (high volume)

02
Movement Family Two
PULL
Back, biceps, and grip — drawing the body toward a surface, or a surface toward the body

Pull movements train the posterior chain of the upper body — the muscles that draw the elbows toward the torso and the shoulder blades together and down. In a world of desks, phones, and hunched posture, these are the muscles most urgently in need of deliberate training. The pull family runs from the dead hang — the honest starting point — through to the full pull-up and chin-up. Every exercise in this family begins with the scapulae depressing and retracting. That sequence never changes.

Pull · Scapular · Exercise 01 of 05
SCAPULAR PULL
The first movement every pull-up beginner must master — before the elbows bend a single degree
Scapular ControlPull-up barBeginner
Frame 1 — Dead Hang
Hanging from a bar, arms fully extended, shoulders shrugged up around the ears. This is the passive hang — the starting point before any scapular work begins.
Muscles Working
Primary
Lower trapezius — pulling the shoulder blades down away from the ears. Serratus anterior — stabilising the scapulae against the ribcage.
Secondary
Latissimus dorsi — beginning to engage in the depressed position. Rhomboids — scapular retraction assisting the pull.
Step by Step
1
Hang passively
Let the shoulders fully shrug up to the ears. Arms completely straight. Feel the full weight of the body hanging from the shoulder joint. This is the passive hang — know what it feels like.
2
Pull the shoulder blades down
Without bending the elbows even a degree, pull the shoulder blades down and back — as if placing them into your back pockets. The body rises slightly as a result.
Feel it: muscles between and below the shoulder blades initiating the movement
3
Hold and return
Hold the depressed position for two seconds. Return slowly to the passive hang. This is one rep. The elbows have not moved at all.
Feel it: the difference between passive and active — this is what initiates every pull-up
Common Folly
Common Folly — Scapular Pull
Bending the elbows to assist the movement.
The arms stay perfectly straight throughout. If the elbows bend, the biceps are compensating for the scapular muscles that haven't yet learned to fire. Slow down and focus on the feeling of blades moving, not arms pulling.
Skipping this exercise because it feels too easy or too basic.
The scapular pull is the foundation of the entire pull family. Those who cannot initiate a pull-up from the shoulder blades — rather than the arms — plateau early and often acquire shoulder impingement. Earn this movement before loading the pull-up.
Progressions
Passive hang only
Simply hanging and building grip and shoulder tolerance. The very first step.
Beginner
Scapular pull ← here
Active depression and retraction from the hang. Target 3×10 slow reps.
Beginner
Dead hang + scap pull into row
Adding a partial elbow bend after the scapular depression — the bridge into the full pull-up.
Intermediate
Fillers when not yet available
Floor scap retractionBand pull-apartPassive hang (grip building)
Pull · Grip and Shoulder · Exercise 02 of 05
DEAD HANG
Two minutes a day decompresses the spine, builds the grip, and prepares the shoulder for everything that follows
Grip and ShoulderPull-up barBeginner
Frame 1 — Passive Hang
Fully relaxed hang from a bar. Shoulders shrugged. Arms straight. The spine elongates under its own weight. This is the passive hang — therapeutic in its own right, and the foundation of all bar work.
Muscles Working
Primary (active hang)
Latissimus dorsi — depressing the scapulae in the active phase. Forearm flexors — maintaining grip throughout.
Stabilising
Rotator cuff — shoulder joint integrity in the overhead position. Core — preventing excessive swinging.
Step by Step
1
Grip the bar
Pronated grip (overhand), hands shoulder-width apart. Full grip — all four fingers and thumb wrapped around the bar. Jump or step up so the arms are straight from the start.
2
Let everything go — passive hang
Allow the shoulders to shrug fully, the spine to elongate, and gravity to do its work. Breathe. Feel the decompression through the lower back and hips.
Feel it: every vertebra creating space below it
3
Activate — the active hang
Pull the shoulder blades down and back without bending the elbows. The body rises slightly. Hold this active position for the remainder of the set time. This is the foundation of the pull-up.
Feel it: lats and lower traps firing, shoulders away from the ears
Common Folly
Common Folly — Dead Hang
Permanently shrugging — never allowing the scapulae to depress into the active position.
Practise the transition deliberately: shrug fully (passive), then pull the blades down (active). Feel the difference. Train both — the passive hang decompresses the spine, the active hang builds the pulling foundation.
Treating the dead hang as a rest between other exercises rather than an exercise in itself.
The dead hang develops grip strength, shoulder health, spinal decompression, and the scapular awareness the pull-up depends on. Two minutes accumulated per day produces measurable change in shoulder health and grip within weeks.
Progressions
Passive hang (timed)
Pure decompression and grip building. Target: 60 seconds continuous.
Beginner
Active hang ← here
Scapular depression held throughout. Target: 3×30 seconds active hold.
Beginner
Weighted hang
Weight belt or dumbbell between the feet. Grip and shoulder strength development.
Intermediate
Fillers when not yet available
Doorframe hangLow bar hang (feet touching)Towel hang (grip builder)
Pull · Horizontal · Exercise 03 of 05
INVERTED ROW
The horizontal pull that most people skip — and the reason most people's pull-up form never improves
Horizontal PullLow bar or tableBeginner → Intermediate
Frame 1 — Starting Position
Lying beneath a low bar or table edge, arms extended, heels on the floor, body straight. This is a horizontal pull — the upper body equivalent of a deadlift in direction of pull.
Muscles Working
Primary
Rhomboids and mid-trapezius — retracting the shoulder blades. Latissimus dorsi — driving the elbows toward the hips.
Secondary
Biceps brachii — elbow flexion assistance. Rear deltoid — horizontal abduction at the shoulder. Core — maintaining the plank.
Step by Step
1
Set up beneath the bar
Grip the bar with an overhand or underhand grip, arms straight. Heels on the floor, body forming a straight line — the inverted plank.
2
Initiate with the shoulder blades
Before the elbows bend, pull the shoulder blades together and down. This is the same initiation as the pull-up — the shoulder blades move before the arms.
Feel it: rhomboids and mid-back contracting first
3
Pull the chest to the bar
Draw the elbows back and pull until the chest touches the bar — not the chin. The chest is the target. Hold one second at the top.
Feel it: chest touching, not chin reaching
4
Lower with control
Two-second return to full arm extension. The lowering phase is where significant strength is built — do not drop.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Inverted Row
The chin leading instead of the chest — pulling the head forward rather than the body upward.
The chest is the target, not the chin. Think of pulling the sternum to the bar. If the chin is reaching, the upper traps are compensating for a weak mid-back. Reduce the height of the bar and focus on the chest arriving first.
Hips sagging — breaking the inverted plank.
Squeeze the glutes and brace the core before every rep. The body is a straight line from heels to shoulders. Sagging hips reduce the demand on the back and transfer load to the lumbar spine.
Skipping this exercise in favour of pull-up attempts before the back is ready.
The inverted row trains the horizontal pull — a different plane to the pull-up but using the same initiating muscles. Those who skip horizontal pulling develop vertical pulling strength on a weak base and plateau earlier.
Progressions
High bar row (easier angle)
Bar set higher reduces the load. Builds the pattern before full bodyweight is demanded.
Beginner
Inverted row ← here
Standard version, heels on floor. Target 3×12 before elevating the feet.
Intermediate
Feet elevated inverted row
Feet on a box or chair, increasing the load toward full bodyweight pulling.
Advanced
Ring row
Rings add instability and allow progressive difficulty adjustment mid-set.
Advanced
Fillers when not yet available
High bar rowResistance band rowTowel door row
Pull · Vertical · Exercise 04 of 05
CHIN-UP
The underhand grip that brings the biceps into the pull — often the more accessible first vertical pull for beginners
Vertical PullPull-up barIntermediate
Frame 1 — Dead Hang, Underhand Grip
Supinated (underhand) grip, hands shoulder-width, arms fully extended, shoulders active. The chin-up begins from a true dead hang — not a partial hang, not a jump-and-hold. Start fully extended, every rep.
Muscles Working
Primary
Latissimus dorsi — primary vertical pulling force. Biceps brachii — significantly more engaged than in the pull-up due to the supinated grip.
Secondary
Rear deltoid — horizontal abduction as the elbows pass the torso. Lower trapezius — scapular depression maintaining the pull.
Step by Step
1
Grip underhand, shoulder-width
Palms facing toward you. Shoulder-width grip. Arms fully straight. Begin with the scapular pull — blades down and back before the elbows bend.
2
Pull the elbows to the hips
Think of driving the elbows down toward the hip bones rather than pulling the body up. This engages the lats rather than over-relying on the biceps.
Feel it: lats contracting, elbows travelling down and back
3
Clear the chin above the bar
Pull until the chin is clearly above the bar. No partial reps. The chin must clear, not just approach.
4
Lower to full extension
Two-second controlled descent to a true dead hang. Do not drop — the negative is where significant strength is built.
Feel it: the lats lengthening under control
Common Folly
Common Folly — Chin-Up
Not starting from a true dead hang — beginning from a partial arm bend.
Every rep starts from full arm extension, shoulders active (blades depressed). Partial range builds partial strength. A chin-up that begins from a bent arm is a significantly easier exercise than the one being trained.
Kipping — using a hip swing to generate momentum.
The kipping chin-up is a different exercise that builds skill in the kip, not strength in the pull. Strict chin-ups only in Foundation Level. Momentum borrowed from the hips is strength the back never develops.
The chin approaching but not clearing the bar.
Chin above the bar is the standard. Not chin at bar level. Not chin near bar. Above. If it won't clear, reduce the reps and build the last few centimetres over time with band assistance.
Progressions
Negative chin-up
Jump to top position and lower as slowly as possible. Builds eccentric strength before the concentric is available.
Beginner
Band-assisted chin-up
Resistance band reduces effective bodyweight. Remove assistance gradually.
Beginner
Full chin-up ← here
Strict, full range. Target 3×8 before adding load.
Intermediate
Weighted chin-up
Belt or dumbbell. The natural progression once bodyweight is mastered.
Advanced

Pull · Vertical · Exercise 05 of 05
PULL-UP
The benchmark of upper body pulling strength — overhand grip, full range, no excuses
Vertical PullPull-up barIntermediate → Advanced
Frame 1 — Dead Hang, Overhand Grip
Pronated (overhand) grip, hands slightly wider than shoulder-width, arms fully extended, scapulae active. The pull-up is harder than the chin-up because the biceps are in a mechanically weaker position. Every rep from a true dead hang.
Muscles Working
Primary
Latissimus dorsi — the dominant mover. The pull-up is primarily a lat exercise. Teres major — assisting the lat throughout.
Secondary
Biceps brachii — less engaged than the chin-up due to pronated grip. Rear deltoid — horizontal abduction. Lower trapezius — scapular control throughout.
Step by Step
1
Set the grip and activate the scapulae
Overhand grip, slightly wider than shoulders. From the dead hang, pull the shoulder blades down and back before the elbows move. This is not optional — it is the foundation of every rep.
Feel it: lats engaging, shoulders away from the ears
2
Drive the elbows toward the floor
Think of pushing the elbows down toward the hip bones, not of pulling the chin up to the bar. This mental cue keeps the lats in the movement rather than turning it into a bicep curl.
3
Pull until the chin clears the bar
The chin must clear the bar cleanly. At the top, the elbows are drawn past the torso, the chest is close to the bar, and the lats are fully contracted.
Feel it: lats fully shortened, chest near bar, chin over
4
Lower to full extension
Three-second negative to a true dead hang. The lowering phase builds strength faster than the pulling phase. Never drop from the bar.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Pull-Up
Kipping — the hip swing that cheats the range.
The kipping pull-up is a gymnastics skill with its own value — it is not a substitute for the strict pull-up and does not build the same strength. In Foundation Level, every pull-up is strict. The swing is borrowed momentum that the back never had to produce.
Chin-over but not chin-clearly-over — the partial rep disguised as a full one.
The chin clears the bar by a visible margin. Not grazes it. Not approaches it. Clears it. If the last few centimetres are impossible, build them with band assistance or negative pull-ups until they are not.
Not going to full extension at the bottom — bouncing off a partial hang.
Every rep starts from a dead hang. Partial reps reduce range by design. They feel like more work while producing less strength development. Full range, every time.
Progressions
Negative pull-up
Jump to the top and lower for 5 seconds. The eccentric builds strength before the concentric is possible.
Beginner
Band-assisted pull-up
Progressive band reduction until the full pull-up is achieved unassisted.
Intermediate
Full pull-up ← here
Strict, full range, overhand. Target 3×8 before adding load.
Intermediate
Weighted pull-up
Weight belt or dumbbell. The direct progression toward the muscle-up.
Advanced
Fillers when not yet available
Negative pull-upBand-assisted pull-upChin-up (easier grip)Inverted row

03
Movement Family Three
LOWER BODY
Quads, glutes, and hamstrings — the engines of the body, trained through the full range they were built to move through

Lower body movements in this programme are built around three patterns: the hinge (glute bridge, Nordic curl), the squat (bodyweight squat, pistol squat, Bulgarian split squat), and the lunge (reverse lunge). Each pattern trains the lower body through a different primary loading angle. Neglect any one and the others compensate — which is how knee problems, hip problems, and lower back problems develop over time. Train all three consistently and the lower body becomes what it was designed to be: the most powerful muscular system in the body.

Lower Body · Hip Extension · Exercise 01 of 07
GLUTE BRIDGE
The exercise most people do without their glutes firing — and the one that fixes it
Hip ExtensionNo equipmentBeginner
Frame 1 — Starting Position
Lying on the back, knees bent to roughly 90 degrees, feet flat on the floor hip-width apart, arms by the sides. The lower back has a natural arch. This will be maintained — not forced flat — as the hips rise.
Muscles Working
Primary
Gluteus maximus — the primary hip extensor. This is the target. If you don't feel it here, something is compensating. Hamstrings — assisting hip extension throughout.
Stabilising
Gluteus medius — preventing the knees from caving inward. Core — spinal stability throughout the movement.
Step by Step
1
Set the feet and activate
Feet hip-width, heels roughly under the knees. Press the feet into the floor and feel the glutes begin to engage before the hips lift.
2
Drive through the heels
Push the heels into the floor and drive the hips upward. The power should come from the glutes, not from pushing through the toes or arching the lower back.
Feel it: glutes contracting to initiate the lift, not the hamstrings pulling
3
Squeeze at the top and hold
At full hip extension, squeeze the glutes hard and hold for two full seconds. The hips, knees, and shoulders form a straight line. Do not hyperextend the lower back.
Feel it: maximum glute contraction held — this is the point of the exercise
4
Lower with control
Two-second descent. Do not rest the hips fully on the floor between reps — maintain tension through the set.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Glute Bridge
Arching the lower back instead of squeezing the glutes — getting height without engaging the target muscle.
At the top, place a hand on the lower back. If it is arched rather than neutral, the lumbar extensors are doing the work that the glutes should be doing. Focus on the squeeze first, the height second. The glutes are what lifts the hips — the back is just along for the ride.
Rushing through the top position without holding the contraction.
The two-second squeeze at the top is not optional. It is the moment the glute is fully contracted and under load. Bouncing through the top position is a warm-up, not an exercise.
Feet too far from the body — reducing glute engagement and increasing hamstring dominance.
Heels should sit roughly under the knees. Move the feet closer and feel the difference in where the exercise is felt. Closer heels increase glute recruitment. Too-far feet shift the work to the hamstrings.
Progressions
Two-leg glute bridge ← here
Standard version. Target 3×20 with a 2-second squeeze before progressing.
Beginner
Single-leg glute bridge
One foot elevated, one leg extended. Doubles the load on each glute and exposes imbalances between sides.
Intermediate
Hip thrust (elevated shoulders)

04
Movement Family Four
CORE
Abs, stability, and anti-extension — the centre that makes every other movement honest

The core is not the six-pack. The six-pack is the surface — the rectus abdominis, one muscle in a much larger system. The core includes the transverse abdominis (the deep compressive layer), the obliques (rotation and lateral stability), the multifidus (spinal stabilisers running the length of the back), the diaphragm, and the pelvic floor. Training the core means training all of these, through their actual functions: resisting extension, resisting rotation, creating intra-abdominal pressure, and transferring force between the upper and lower body. Every exercise in this family trains at least one of these functions. Master them in order — the hollow body hold first, always.

Rectus abdominis — the flexion force drawing ribcage and pelvis together. Transverse abdominis — the deep compressive layer creating intra-abdominal pressure.

Hip flexors — holding the legs elevated. Quadriceps — keeping legs straight under load. Serratus anterior — scapular depression and protraction.

Holding the breath to maintain the position.
The position must be maintainable while breathing continuously. Breath-holding creates false intra-abdominal pressure masking genuine core weakness. If you cannot breathe in the hollow, reduce the difficulty until you can.
Legs too high — avoiding the point of the exercise.
Legs at 90 degrees require very little core work. Lower the legs progressively until the position is genuinely demanding with the back still flat. That height is your working position.

Rotate the legs to one side
While keeping the legs at horizontal, rotate them toward one side — as far as control allows. The upper body stays still. Only the legs move.
Feel it: obliques loading heavily as the legs pass the midline
3
Control the return and cross to the other side
Return to centre under control — do not allow gravity to swing the legs back. Then rotate to the opposite side. This is one full repetition.
Feel it: the obliques working hardest on the controlled return — never swing
Common Folly
Common Folly — Windshield Wiper
Using momentum to swing the legs from side to side rather than controlling the rotation.
The obliques work hardest on the controlled lowering — not the return. If the legs swing freely, gravity is doing the work the obliques should be doing. Slow the rotation down until each side takes at least three seconds.
Allowing the legs to drop below horizontal during the rotation.
The legs must remain at horizontal throughout the full arc. Dropping below horizontal reduces the rotational demand and turns this into a pendulum swing. Bend the knees if necessary to maintain the height.
Progressions
Lying windshield wiper
On the back, arms out to the sides, legs raised and rotated to the floor. Lower gravity demand than the hanging version.
Intermediate
Bent-knee hanging wiper ← here
Hanging, knees bent at 90 degrees. Reduced lever arm — more manageable oblique demand.
Advanced
Straight-leg hanging wiper
Full straight-leg rotation. Maximum lever arm, maximum oblique demand.

Core · Advanced Isometric / Dynamic · Exercise 07 of 07
DRAGON FLAG
The exercise Bruce Lee made famous — the entire body held horizontal, the core the only thing between you and the floor
Advanced CoreBench or anchorAdvanced
Frame 1 — Anchor Setup
Lying on a bench, gripping the bench edge behind the head with both hands. The grip is the anchor. Everything below the shoulders will be raised and held horizontal — the entire body as a rigid lever extending from the shoulder pivot point.
Muscles Working
Primary
Entire anterior core — rectus abdominis, transverse abdominis, obliques working in concert to maintain the rigid hollow body position against the full lever arm of the body.
Secondary
Latissimus dorsi — creating tension through the torso and into the grip. Hip flexors — maintaining leg position in the flag. Glutes — keeping the hips extended, not flexed.
Step by Step
1
Establish the hollow body position first
Before attempting the dragon flag, the hollow body hold must be a mastered exercise. The dragon flag is the hollow body held vertically — if the shape breaks, the flag fails. There are no shortcuts through this prerequisite.
2
Press the upper back into the bench and raise the body
Gripping the bench edge, use the lats and core to press the upper back into the bench and swing the hips and legs upward until the body is vertical. Enter the hollow body position immediately.
3
Lower the body in the hollow position
From vertical, lower the body slowly toward horizontal — maintaining the hollow body shape as one rigid unit. The hips must not break (sag) as the body descends. Lower only as far as the shape can be maintained.
Feel it: the whole anterior core resisting the descent as one unit
4
Raise and repeat
Return to vertical under control. Begin with 3–5 controlled negatives before attempting the full dynamic version.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Dragon Flag
Attempting the dragon flag before the hollow body hold is mastered.
The dragon flag is the hollow body hold with a 90-degree rotation and the full bodyweight lever against the core. If the hollow body hold breaks in the first 20 seconds, the dragon flag is not yet appropriate. Master the prerequisite first — completely, not approximately.
The hips breaking (sagging) as the body is lowered — piking rather than flagging.
The hips must stay fully extended throughout — the body is one rigid line from shoulders to toes. Any hip sag dramatically reduces the lever arm and removes the core demand. If the hips break, reduce the range immediately.
Crashing down rather than controlling the descent.
The eccentric — the controlled lowering — is where the dragon flag builds the most strength. Begin with negatives only: raise the body and lower as slowly as possible. The concentric (raising) comes later.
Progressions
Tuck dragon flag
Knees tucked to chest, dramatically reducing the lever arm. The entry point to the movement pattern.
Intermediate
Negative dragon flag
Full body raised to vertical and lowered as slowly as possible. Eccentric only — no concentric yet.
Advanced
Full dragon flag ← here
Complete concentric and eccentric, body horizontal at the lowest point. Target 3×5 controlled reps.

Movement Family Five
SKILL
Handstand and muscle-up — the aspirational movements that prove the whole system has worked

Skill movements are different in character from the other four families. They are not primarily about muscular strength — though they demand it. They are about the nervous system learning a new motor pattern: balance, timing, proprioception, and the willingness to be upside down or to trust an explosive transition. The handstand hold and the muscle-up are the aspirational movements of Foundation Level. They appear in the programme as targets. The exercises in the other four families are what make them possible. Arrive at these movements having done the work — and they will come.

Skill · Inverted Balance · Exercise 01 of 02
HANDSTAND HOLD
The inverted hollow body — balance skill, shoulder strength, and body awareness combined in one position
Inverted BalanceWall to startIntermediate → Advanced
Frame 1 — Wall Setup
Hands placed roughly shoulder-width, 15–20cm from the wall. The wall-assisted handstand is the starting point — not a lesser exercise, but the correct progression. The goal is to use the wall less over time, not to avoid it.
Muscles Working
Primary
Anterior deltoid — pressing overhead under full bodyweight. Triceps — elbow stability in full extension. Wrist flexors — balance through finger pressure on the floor.
Secondary
Core (hollow body position) — the handstand is the hollow body hold inverted. The same shape, the same tension, 180 degrees rotated. Scapular stabilisers — shoulder girdle control throughout.
Step by Step
1
Set the hands — fingertips pressing
Hands shoulder-width, fingers spread, 15–20cm from the wall. The fingers are the fine balance control — pressing the fingertips into the floor corrects a forward tip; pressing the heel of the hand corrects backward tipping.
Feel it: the hands are not passive supports — they are active balance tools
2
Kick up to the wall
From a lunge position, kick the back leg up toward the wall and let the front leg follow. The body arrives in a vertical position with the heels resting lightly against the wall. The arms are straight throughout.
3
Build the hollow body position inverted
Posteriorly tilt the pelvis, pull the ribs down, squeeze the glutes. The handstand is the hollow body hold rotated 180 degrees. Without this shape, the body will arch and the balance will be impossible.
Feel it: the same tension as the hollow body hold — abs, glutes, lats all working
4
Push away from the wall incrementally
Over weeks, encourage the hips to move away from the wall slightly. Balance for longer periods without touching the wall. The freestanding handstand is built this way — incrementally, not suddenly.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Handstand Hold
Banana back — arching the lower back instead of maintaining the hollow body position.
The arch is the most common handstand error. It makes the position feel more stable against the wall but makes the freestanding handstand impossible. Learn the hollow body handstand from day one — posterior pelvic tilt, ribs down, straight line from wrist to ankle.
Arms bending under the load — losing the locked-out elbow position.
The arms must be fully extended and externally rotated throughout. Bent arms in a handstand are a shoulder injury waiting to happen. Build sufficient pike push-up and dip strength before loading the handstand with significant volume.
Using the wall as a permanent crutch rather than a temporary support.
The wall teaches the position. After that, it must be progressively removed. Set a timer and try to hold without touching the wall for increasing periods. One second freestanding becomes five, becomes ten, becomes thirty.
Kicking up too hard and overbalancing — not controlling the entry.
The kick-up should be controlled — enough to reach vertical, not enough to fly past it. Practise the kick-up separately until it consistently arrives at vertical without overshooting.
Progressions
Wall handstand hold
Heels against the wall, hollow body position. Target 3×30 seconds before attempting to leave the wall.
Intermediate
Chest-to-wall handstand
Facing the wall (chest toward it). Forces the hollow body position — the back cannot arch into the wall.
Intermediate
Freestanding handstand ← here
Away from the wall. Target 10 seconds unbroken before progressing to handstand push-ups.
Advanced

Skill · Explosive Pull / Push Transition · Exercise 02 of 02
MUSCLE-UP
The movement that proves the pull family and the push family have both been mastered — a transition that cannot be faked
Explosive Pull / PushPull-up barAdvanced
Frame 1 — Dead Hang
Hanging from a pull-up bar, overhand grip slightly wider than shoulder-width. The muscle-up begins exactly as the pull-up does — from a dead hang with active scapulae. The difference arrives at the top of the pull: instead of stopping at chin-over-bar, the body transitions above the bar.
Muscles Working
Primary
Latissimus dorsi — the explosive pull phase. Triceps — the push phase after the transition. Anterior deltoid — the transition itself, pressing the body above the bar.
Secondary
Biceps — pull phase assistance. Core (hollow body) — the transition requires the body to be rigid or the movement fails. Wrist flexors — transitioning the wrist over the bar.
Step by Step
1
Initiate an explosive pull
From the dead hang, initiate the pull with the scapulae as usual — then pull explosively and as high as possible. The goal is to pull the bar to the hips, not just the chin to the bar. Lean slightly back as you pull to help the bar path clear the chest.
Feel it: pulling the bar toward the hips — not the chin toward the bar
2
Transition over the bar
At the peak of the pull, the wrists rotate over the bar and the elbows drive forward and down. The chest passes above the bar. This is the transition — a single fluid movement, not a pause followed by a push.
Feel it: pull and transition are one movement — there is no stopping point between them
3
Press to full lockout
Once above the bar, press to full arm extension as in a dip. The body is now above the bar, arms straight. This is the top position.
4
Lower with control
Reverse the movement — lower through the dip, transition back below the bar, and descend to the dead hang. The negative muscle-up is where significant strength is built.
Common Folly
Common Folly — Muscle-Up
Attempting the muscle-up before achieving at least 8–10 strict pull-ups and 10 strict dips.
The muscle-up is a pull-up combined with a dip. Both must be independently strong before the combination is attempted. There is no shortcut through this prerequisite — those who rush it develop a muscle-up that is neither a proper pull nor a proper dip, and plateau at a low number permanently.
Treating the pull and the transition as two separate movements with a pause between them.
The pull, transition, and push are one continuous movement. Any pause at the bar breaks the momentum required for the transition. The muscle-up is a fluid sequence — it must be trained as such from the first attempt.
Excessive kipping — relying on a hip swing to generate the momentum the pulling strength should provide.
A small hip hollow-to-arch swing is acceptable in the strict muscle-up for timing. Excessive kipping is a compensation for insufficient pull strength. Build the strict pull-up to 10+ reps before using any kip — the kip then becomes a timing tool rather than a crutch.
The false grip causing the wrist to fail at the transition.
The false grip — where the wrist sits over the bar rather than under it — makes the transition significantly more accessible. Learn the false grip on the dead hang before attempting the muscle-up. It is uncomfortable at first and worth the discomfort entirely.
Progressions
Explosive pull-up (bar to chest)
Pull as high as possible — bar reaching the chest or lower. Builds the pull strength required for the transition.
Intermediate
Negative muscle-up
Begin above the bar and lower through the transition to a dead hang as slowly as possible. Builds eccentric familiarity with the movement pattern.
Advanced
Band-assisted muscle-up
Resistance band provides assistance through the transition. Reduce band thickness progressively.
Advanced
Full muscle-up ← here
Strict, unassisted, controlled. One fluid movement from dead hang to full lockout above the bar.
Advanced
